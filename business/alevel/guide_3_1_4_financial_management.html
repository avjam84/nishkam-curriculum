3.1.4 2.1 Managing Finance: Objectives and Influences

Financial objectives

Financial objectives are specific, measurable targets that the finance function aims to achieve. Like all objectives, they should be SMART (Specific, Measurable, Accountable, Realistic, Time-specific).

ObjectiveWhat it means
ProfitEarning more revenue than it costs to run the business — the fundamental objective for most commercial businesses
ProfitabilityThe relationship between profit and revenue or capital employed — a business can increase profit without improving profitability if costs rise at the same rate
Cash flowEnsuring the business has enough cash coming in to meet its obligations as they fall due — a profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash
LiquidityHaving sufficient short-term assets (cash, receivables) to cover short-term liabilities — measured by the current ratio and acid test ratio
Borrowing levelsManaging the amount of debt relative to equity — too much borrowing increases risk and interest costs, but some borrowing can fund growth

Influences on financial objectives

Business ownership — A plc may prioritise shareholder returns. A sole trader may prioritise personal income. A social enterprise may prioritise covering costs rather than maximising profit.

Business size — A start-up may prioritise survival and cash flow. An established business may prioritise profitability and growth.

Market conditions — During a recession, survival and cash flow may take priority over profit growth. In a booming economy, a business may target rapid expansion.

Stakeholder expectations — Shareholders expect returns. Banks expect loan repayments. Employees expect job security. These competing demands shape financial objectives.

Interrelationship with other functions

Finance is not a standalone function. It provides the data and resources that other functions need to operate:

• Marketing — Marketing needs a budget. Revenue forecasts from marketing feed into financial plans. Pricing decisions directly affect revenue and margins.

• Operations — Operations decisions (capacity, stock levels, technology investment) have major cost implications. Finance must fund capital investment and manage working capital.

• People — Staff costs are usually the largest single expense. Decisions about recruitment, training, and pay directly affect profitability and cash flow.

Common mistake
The finance function produces calculations and financial information for management to use in decision-making. It does not make the investment or strategic decisions itself — those are made by directors and senior managers using the financial data alongside other information.

Competitiveness

Good financial management contributes to competitiveness by ensuring the business has sufficient resources to invest, respond to market changes, and withstand economic downturns. A business with strong cash flow and low gearing has more flexibility than one that is heavily indebted.

3.1.4 2.2 Sources of Finance

Internal sources

SourceWhat it isConsiderations
Owners’ investmentMoney put into the business by the owner(s)No interest or repayment. But the owner bears all the risk, and they may have limited personal funds.
Retained profitsProfits kept in the business rather than paid out to owners or shareholdersNo cost of borrowing. But reduces dividends, which may make shareholders unhappy. Only available if the business is already profitable.
Sale of assetsSelling things the business owns (property, vehicles, equipment) to raise cashQuick source of funds. But the business loses the use of the asset, and may get less than market value if selling under pressure.
Sale and leasebackSelling an asset (usually property) and immediately leasing it back from the buyerReleases cash while retaining use of the asset. But commits the business to ongoing lease payments, and the asset is no longer owned.
Working capitalUsing the cash tied up in day-to-day operations — reducing stock, collecting debts faster, delaying payments to suppliersNo external cost. But may strain relationships with suppliers or customers, and reducing stock risks shortages.

External sources

SourceWhat it isConsiderations
Trade creditBuying goods or services from suppliers now and paying later (typically 30–90 days)Short-term, no interest if paid on time. But losing early payment discounts is an opportunity cost, and late payment damages supplier relationships.
Share capitalRaising money by selling shares in the businessNo repayment or interest. But dilutes ownership and control. Only available to limited companies.
OverdraftSpending more than is in the bank account, up to an agreed limitFlexible — only pay interest on what you use. But interest rates are high, and the bank can withdraw the facility at any time.
LoansBorrowing a fixed amount to be repaid over an agreed period with interestPredictable repayments for budgeting. But interest is a cost, and the business may need to provide security (collateral).
Business angelsWealthy individuals who invest in small or start-up businesses in exchange for equityBring money, expertise, and contacts. But they take a share of ownership and often want involvement in decisions.
Private equityInvestment firms that buy significant stakes in businesses, often taking them privateLarge amounts of capital available. But private equity investors typically want significant control and expect high returns, often within 3–7 years.
Crowd fundingRaising small amounts of money from a large number of people, typically through online platformsCan also be a marketing tool and test of demand. But not all campaigns succeed, and managing many small investors can be complex.

Suitability

The right source of finance depends on the purpose (short-term working capital vs long-term investment), the amount needed, the type of business (sole traders cannot sell shares), the existing level of debt, and the owner’s willingness to give up control. Matching the source to the purpose is essential — using a short-term overdraft to fund a long-term investment is dangerous.

Example
In the SAM Paper 1 Q1.5, students evaluate which source of external finance Gymshark should use to fund ambitious growth plans. The indicative content discusses loans, share capital, and private equity — weighing the cost, control implications, and suitability of each for a fast-growing business.

3.1.4 2.3 Break-Even Analysis

Key terms and formulas

TermFormula / Definition
Fixed costsCosts that do not change with output in the short term (rent, salaries, insurance)
Variable costsCosts that change in direct proportion to output (raw materials, packaging, direct labour per unit)
Total costsFixed costs + total variable costs
RevenueSelling price per unit × quantity sold
Contribution per unitSelling price per unit − variable cost per unit
Total contributionContribution per unit × number of units sold (or: revenue − total variable costs)
Break-even outputFixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
Margin of safetyActual (or expected) output − break-even output
Definition
Break-even is the level of output at which total revenue equals total costs — the business is making neither a profit nor a loss. The margin of safety is how far above break-even the business is currently operating — the bigger the margin of safety, the less vulnerable the business is to a fall in demand.

Break-even charts

A break-even chart shows fixed costs, total costs, and total revenue plotted against output. The break-even point is where the total costs and total revenue lines cross. The area to the left of this point represents losses; the area to the right represents profits.

Effects of changes in price, output and costs

You should be able to show and explain how changes in selling price, variable costs, or fixed costs affect the break-even point and margin of safety.

• Price increase → contribution per unit rises → break-even output falls → margin of safety increases

• Variable cost increase → contribution per unit falls → break-even output rises → margin of safety decreases

• Fixed cost increase → break-even output rises → margin of safety decreases

Purpose and value of break-even analysis

Break-even analysis is a useful planning tool. It helps a business understand the minimum sales needed to cover costs, assess the impact of price or cost changes, and support decisions about whether to launch a new product. However, it has limitations:

• It assumes all output is sold, which may not be realistic.

• It assumes fixed costs remain constant, but in reality they may change as output increases significantly (stepped fixed costs).

• It assumes a single product or a constant sales mix, which is rarely true for businesses with multiple products.

• It assumes the selling price stays the same at all output levels, but in practice a business may need to lower prices to sell more.

Note
The limitations of break-even analysis are natural evaluation points. If a question asks you to use break-even data, calculate accurately and interpret it — but then question the assumptions behind the analysis.

3.1.4 2.4 Profit

Types of profit

TypeFormulaWhat it tells you
Gross profitRevenue − cost of salesHow much profit the business makes from its core trading activity before overhead costs. Reflects pricing and production efficiency.
Operating profitGross profit − operating expensesHow much profit the business makes from its normal operations, after all day-to-day costs. Reflects how well the business is managed overall.
Profit for the yearOperating profit − interest − tax (+ any exceptional items)The final profit available to shareholders. Affected by financing decisions (interest) and tax obligations.

Profit vs cash

Common mistake
Profit and cash are not the same thing. A business can be profitable but run out of cash (if customers have not yet paid, or if large capital investments have drained the bank account). Equally, a business can have cash in the bank but be making a loss (if it has just received a large loan or sold an asset). Profit is an accounting measure. Cash is actual money in the bank. Students who confuse these will lose marks.

Profit vs profitability

Profit is an absolute number (e.g. £500,000). Profitability is a ratio that puts profit in context (e.g. profit relative to revenue or capital employed). A business can increase profit while profitability stays the same or even falls — if revenue or capital employed grew faster than profit.

Ways of increasing gross and operating profits

There are two fundamental approaches:

Increasing revenue — Raising prices (if demand is inelastic enough), increasing sales volume through better marketing or new products, entering new markets, or improving the product to justify higher prices.

Decreasing costs — Negotiating lower prices from suppliers, improving operational efficiency, reducing waste, automating processes, or rationalising the workforce. However, cost-cutting can damage quality, morale, or customer service if taken too far.

3.1.4 2.5 Budgets

Budgeting

Definition
A budget is a financial plan for a future period, setting out expected income and expenditure. Budgets allow managers to plan, coordinate activities across the business, delegate spending authority, monitor performance, and take corrective action when actual results differ from the plan.

Zero-based budgeting

In zero-based budgeting, every budget starts from zero each period. Every item of expenditure must be justified from scratch, rather than simply adjusting last year’s budget. This forces managers to think carefully about what they really need to spend, and can eliminate wasteful spending that has been carried forward from year to year. However, it is time-consuming and can be demotivating for managers who must fight for every item of expenditure.

Budget variances

Definition
A variance is the difference between the budgeted figure and the actual figure. A favourable variance is one that improves profit (actual revenue higher than budgeted, or actual costs lower than budgeted). An adverse variance is one that reduces profit (actual revenue lower than budgeted, or actual costs higher than budgeted).
ScenarioVariance type
Revenue is higher than budgetFavourable
Revenue is lower than budgetAdverse
Costs are lower than budgetFavourable
Costs are higher than budgetAdverse
Important
A favourable variance is not always good news, and an adverse variance is not always bad news. A favourable cost variance might mean the business under-invested in marketing or maintenance. An adverse cost variance might be caused by unexpectedly high demand that required extra production — which could actually be positive. Always look at the reason behind the variance.

3.1.4 2.6 Cash Flow and Liquidity

Key terms

Payables — Money the business owes to suppliers (trade payables). These are amounts the business has received goods or services for but has not yet paid.

Receivables — Money owed to the business by customers (trade receivables). These are amounts the business has invoiced but has not yet received payment for.

Common mistake
Students frequently confuse payables and receivables. Payables = the business owes money (a liability). Receivables = the business is owed money (an asset). Remember: you receive from receivables.

Cash flow forecasting

A cash flow forecast predicts the expected cash inflows and outflows over a future period (usually month by month). It helps the business plan for periods when cash may be tight and arrange finance (such as an overdraft) in advance.

You should be able to complete a cash flow forecast (fill in missing figures), interpret what it shows (when will cash be tight? which months have the highest outflows?), and analyse what the forecast means for the business’s financial planning.

Ways of improving cash flow

MethodHow it helpsPotential drawback
Debt factoringSelling receivables to a factoring company for immediate cash (at a discount)The business receives less than the full value of the debt. Customers may view it negatively.
Shortening payment timeAsking customers to pay more quickly (e.g. 14 days instead of 30)May lose customers who prefer longer payment terms, especially in B2B markets.
Early payment incentivesOffering discounts to customers who pay early (e.g. 2% discount for payment within 10 days)Reduces the amount received per invoice. Only works if customers take up the offer.
Credit checksChecking the creditworthiness of customers before offering trade credit, reducing bad debtsMay slow down the process of onboarding new customers.
Increasing payment time to suppliersNegotiating longer payment terms (e.g. 60 days instead of 30) keeps cash in the business longerMay damage supplier relationships or lose early payment discounts.
Reducing outgoingsCutting unnecessary spending to reduce cash flowing outMay cut investment needed for growth, or reduce quality.
Better invoice managementSending invoices promptly, chasing late payments, using automated systemsRequires time and resource, but this is generally good practice with few downsides.

Liquidity ratios

RatioFormulaWhat it tells you
Current ratioCurrent assets ÷ current liabilitiesWhether the business can cover its short-term debts with its short-term assets. A ratio of around 1.5–2 is often considered healthy, though this varies by industry.
Acid test ratio(Current assets − inventories) ÷ current liabilitiesA stricter test that excludes stock (because stock may not be easily converted to cash). A ratio of 1 or above suggests the business can pay its short-term debts without relying on selling stock.
Payables daysTrade payables ÷ cost of sales × 365How many days on average the business takes to pay its suppliers. Higher = the business is holding on to its cash longer.
Receivables daysTrade receivables ÷ revenue × 365How many days on average the business takes to collect payment from customers. Lower = cash is coming in faster.
Example
In the SAM Paper 1 Q1.4, students calculate Gymshark’s current ratio: £142,383 ÷ £192,064 = 0.74. This is below 1, which means Gymshark’s current liabilities exceed its current assets. The question asks whether this suggests a strong financial position — the answer depends on context: fast-growing online retailers often operate with low current ratios because their cash cycle is short (customers pay immediately online, but suppliers are paid on credit terms).

3.1.4 2.7 Financial Reporting

Note
The specification says you must be able to interpret and amend the income statement and statement of financial position. This means you may be given a partially completed financial statement and asked to fill in missing figures, correct errors, or adjust figures to reflect a change (such as an increase in costs or revenue). Analysis of financial data should include index numbers, calculating percentages and percentage changes.

Income statement

The income statement (profit and loss account) shows the business’s revenue, costs, and profit over a period (usually one year). It allows stakeholders to see where the money came from and where it went.

Key lines: revenue → minus cost of sales = gross profit → minus operating expenses = operating profit → minus interest and tax = profit for the year.

Statement of financial position (balance sheet)

The statement of financial position is a snapshot of what the business owns (assets) and what it owes (liabilities) at a specific point in time. It always balances: total assets = total liabilities + equity.

Key sections:

• Non-current assets — Long-term assets such as property, equipment, and vehicles

• Current assets — Short-term assets such as inventory, trade receivables, and cash

• Current liabilities — Short-term debts due within one year (trade payables, overdraft, short-term loans)

• Non-current liabilities — Long-term debts due after more than one year (loans, mortgages)

• Equity — The owners’ stake in the business (share capital + retained profits)

Profitability ratios

RatioFormulaWhat it tells you
Gross profit marginGross profit ÷ revenue × 100The percentage of revenue left after cost of sales. Higher = better pricing or lower production costs.
Operating profit marginOperating profit ÷ revenue × 100The percentage of revenue left after all operating costs. Higher = the business is controlling its overheads well.
Profit for the year marginProfit for the year ÷ revenue × 100The final percentage of revenue that the business keeps as profit after interest and tax.
ROCEOperating profit ÷ (total equity + non-current liabilities) × 100The return the business generates on all the long-term capital invested in it. A key measure of overall business performance. Should exceed the cost of borrowing.
ROIProfit from the investment ÷ cost of the investment × 100The percentage return on a specific investment. Used to compare whether one investment is more worthwhile than another.

Gearing

Definition
Gearing measures the proportion of a business’s long-term finance that comes from debt rather than equity.

Formula: Non-current liabilities ÷ (total equity + non-current liabilities) × 100

A high gearing ratio (above 50%) means the business is heavily reliant on borrowed money. This increases risk because interest must be paid regardless of profit levels, and the debt must eventually be repaid. However, debt can be cheaper than equity (interest payments are tax-deductible), and borrowing allows the business to grow without diluting ownership.

Trends, benchmarking and context

Financial ratios are most useful when compared — against previous years (trend analysis), against competitors (benchmarking), and in the context of the industry, economic conditions, and the business’s strategy. A gross profit margin of 20% might be excellent for a supermarket but poor for a software company.

Limitations of financial reporting

Historic data — Financial statements show what has already happened, not what will happen. They are backward-looking.

Non-financial factors — Financial statements do not capture employee morale, brand strength, customer satisfaction, or environmental impact — all of which affect long-term performance.

3.1.4 2.8 Assessing Financial Position and Performance

The specification says you should be able to assess financial position and performance using the following measures, relative to objectives, competitors, business planning and context.

MeasureWhat it assesses
Profits and marginsIs the business earning enough? Are margins improving or declining?
ROCEIs the business generating a good return on the capital invested?
ROIIs a specific investment generating a worthwhile return?
Cash flow and liquidityCan the business pay its bills? Is cash flow positive and sustainable?
GearingHow reliant is the business on debt? Is this level of risk appropriate?
Shareholder rewards — dividend per share, dividend yieldAre shareholders being adequately rewarded for their investment?
Break-even output and margin of safetyHow close is the business to the point where it starts making a loss?
Budget varianceIs the business performing as planned?

Dividend per share and dividend yield

Dividend per share = total dividends paid ÷ number of shares.

Dividend yield = dividend per share ÷ current share price × 100. This tells investors what percentage return they are getting from dividends relative to the price they paid for the shares.

Note
When assessing financial performance, always consider the context. A falling profit margin might be a concern — or it might be a deliberate strategy (the business is cutting prices to build market share). The numbers alone do not tell you whether the business is performing well. You need to interpret them in light of the business’s objectives, market conditions, and strategic direction.

3.1.4 2.9 Ethics in Finance A-level only

Ethical issues in financial management

Tax avoidance

Tax avoidance is the legal use of the tax system to minimise the amount of tax paid — for example, by shifting profits to low-tax countries, using allowances and reliefs, or structuring transactions to reduce liability. It is legal but increasingly seen as unethical, particularly for large multinational corporations. Companies like Amazon, Google, and Starbucks have faced significant public criticism and reputational damage for paying very little tax in countries where they make large profits.

Payment terms for customers and suppliers

Large businesses sometimes impose long payment terms on suppliers (90 or even 120 days), effectively using their suppliers as a source of free credit. While this improves the large business’s cash flow, it can cause serious cash flow problems for smaller suppliers and has been described as unethical. Conversely, demanding very short payment terms from customers can damage relationships.

Important
Ethics in finance links to business objectives and ownership structure. A plc under pressure from shareholders to maximise short-term profits may be more inclined to use aggressive tax avoidance than a family-owned business that values its local reputation. This is a useful evaluation point.

Traps 4. Common Mistakes

Mistakes to avoid
1. Confusing profit and cash — Profit is an accounting measure (revenue minus costs). Cash is actual money available. A business can be profitable and still run out of cash if it has not collected payments from customers, or if it has spent heavily on assets.

2. Confusing profit and profitability — Profit is an absolute number. Profitability is a ratio. A business can increase profit while profitability falls (if costs grew even faster).

3. Confusing payables and receivables — Payables = money the business owes (a liability). Receivables = money owed to the business (an asset). You receive from receivables.

4. Stating ratios without interpreting them — Calculating a current ratio of 0.74 is only the first step. What does it mean? Is it good or bad? Compared to what? In what industry? Always interpret and contextualise.

5. Assuming a low current ratio is always bad — Some businesses (supermarkets, online retailers) operate successfully with low current ratios because they receive cash from customers immediately but pay suppliers on credit. Context matters.

6. Forgetting the Own Figure Rule — If you make a calculation error, you can still get marks for correctly interpreting your wrong answer — but only if you show your working. Always show the formula, the numbers, and the calculation.

7. Listing sources of finance without evaluating suitability — The question is not “what sources exist?” but “which is most suitable for this business in this situation?” Consider the purpose, amount, ownership structure, existing debt levels, and the business’s willingness to give up control.

8. Assuming favourable variances are always good — A favourable cost variance might mean the business under-spent on something important (marketing, maintenance, training). Always look at the reason behind the variance.

9. Treating break-even as a complete answer — Break-even analysis has significant limitations (assumes all output is sold, constant prices, single product). Use it as a starting point and then evaluate its reliability.

Technique 5. How It Comes Up

Financial 9-mark questions may require a calculation as part of the analysis. Structure: FOR → AGAINST → weighted judgement, with the calculation woven into the application throughout.

Example from the SAMs:

15-mark Evaluate questions (including quantitative)

The most demanding questions. In the SAMs, Q1.5 asks students to evaluate which source of external finance Gymshark should use for its growth plans, and Q2.5 asks students to evaluate which regional market to launch in using ROI calculations. Both options must be analysed and evaluated, with a clear recommendation.

Examples from the SAMs:

• “Evaluate which source of external finance Gymshark should use to fund ambitious growth plans”

• “Evaluate which regional market Apple should launch Vision Pro in to maximise ROI”

Note on quantitative 9-mark and 15-mark questions
When a question requires a calculation, 6 of the marks are for quantitative skills: AO1 (2 marks) for the formula and selecting the right figures, AO2 (2 marks) for performing the calculation, AO3 (1 mark) for interpreting the result, and AO4 (1 mark) for making a judgement based on the calculation. The remaining marks are for qualitative analysis and evaluation. Always show your working — the Own Figure Rule means you can get marks for correctly interpreting a wrong answer.

6-mark Analyse: “Analyse how changes in Apple’s share price could affect the business.” [6 marks] — SAM Paper 1 Q2.2

Show model answer

A company’s share price reflects the market’s valuation of the business. When the share price rises, the company’s market capitalisation increases, which strengthens its financial position and reputation in the eyes of investors, lenders, and other stakeholders. Apple’s share price rose from $150 to approximately $230 over the period shown in Source B, representing a significant increase in market capitalisation for a company of Apple’s size. This means that Apple’s ability to raise additional finance is strengthened, because a rising share price makes it easier and cheaper to issue new shares — existing shareholders are more willing to buy additional shares if the price has been rising, and the company needs to issue fewer shares to raise the same amount of capital. As a result, Apple could use this financial strength to fund large-scale investments such as the Vision Pro launch, which reportedly cost over $3 billion, which could lead to faster product development and market entry, thereby strengthening Apple’s long-term competitive position by allowing it to invest in growth without increasing its borrowing and the associated interest costs.

What makes this work
One clear chain from share price rise → stronger financial position → easier to raise capital → can fund specific investments → competitive advantage. Applied to Apple throughout, with specific data from the case study. Reaches the endpoint (how it affects the business).

9-mark Assess (quantitative): “Assess whether Gymshark’s current ratio suggests a strong financial position.” [9 marks] — SAM Paper 1 Q1.4

Calculation first
Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities
= £142,383 ÷ £192,064
= 0.74

Step 1 — Argument that the current ratio suggests a WEAK financial position

Show model answer: Argument for (weak position)

On one hand, one could argue that Gymshark’s current ratio suggests a weak financial position. The current ratio measures a business’s ability to pay its short-term debts using its short-term assets. A ratio below 1 means that the business does not have enough current assets to cover its current liabilities. Gymshark’s current ratio is 0.74, calculated as £142,383 ÷ £192,064. This means that for every £1 of short-term debt, Gymshark has only 74p of short-term assets to cover it, which suggests that the business could struggle to pay suppliers, tax bills, or other short-term obligations if they all fell due at the same time. As a result, Gymshark may need to arrange additional short-term finance such as an overdraft to cover potential cash shortfalls, which would increase interest costs and reduce profitability. Given that Gymshark’s current liabilities of £192,064 exceed its current assets by almost £50,000, this shows that there is a meaningful gap between what the business owes in the short term and what it can quickly convert to cash.

Step 2 — Argument that the current ratio does NOT necessarily suggest a weak financial position

Show model answer: Argument against (not necessarily weak)

On the other hand, one might argue that Gymshark’s current ratio does not necessarily indicate a weak financial position. The appropriate current ratio varies significantly by industry and business model. Businesses that receive cash from customers quickly but pay suppliers on credit can operate comfortably with current ratios below 1. Gymshark is an online-first retailer — the majority of its sales are through its website, where customers pay by card at the point of purchase. This means Gymshark receives cash almost immediately, while paying suppliers on standard trade credit terms of 30–60 days. This means that the cash cycle is very short, and the business is constantly receiving new cash inflows that do not appear as current assets on the balance sheet date, because by that point they have already been spent on growth and reinvestment. As a result, a snapshot of current assets at one point in time may understate Gymshark’s true ability to meet its obligations, which suggests that the low current ratio reflects the nature of the business model rather than genuine financial weakness. Gymshark’s rapid revenue growth — from a garage start-up to a business valued at over £1 billion — also shows that the business has been able to fund its operations and growth despite a consistently low current ratio, which challenges the assumption that a ratio below 1 is automatically a problem.

Step 3 — Weighted judgement

Show model answer: Weighted judgement

Overall, Gymshark’s current ratio of 0.74 does raise some concerns about short-term liquidity, but it does not necessarily suggest a weak financial position. Although the ratio is below 1, the argument that it reflects the nature of an online direct-to-consumer business model carries greater weight because Gymshark’s cash cycle is fundamentally different from a traditional retailer with large amounts of stock and slow-paying customers. However, this ultimately depends on whether Gymshark’s sales continue to grow at a rate that sustains positive cash flow — if sales growth were to slow or reverse, the low current ratio could become a genuine problem because the constant inflow of customer payments that currently covers short-term liabilities would weaken. Therefore, Gymshark’s current ratio suggests a financial position that is acceptable for its current business model, but one that would need close monitoring if growth were to slow.

What makes these answers work
6-mark: One clear chain from share price rise to competitive benefit, applied to Apple throughout with specific data.

9-mark (quantitative): The calculation is done first and referenced throughout both arguments. FOR and AGAINST both use case study data manipulated into the reasoning. The weighted judgement weighs both sides, comes down on one with a clear condition, and the final sentence answers the question in its own words. The answer challenges the conventional assumption (low current ratio = bad) using contextual analysis.

Examples 8. Real-World Examples

Real-world examples
Current ratio and business model: Supermarkets like Tesco typically operate with current ratios well below 1 (Tesco’s is often around 0.7). This works because customers pay in cash or by card at the till, while suppliers are paid on 30–60 day credit terms. The constant flow of customer payments funds operations day to day, even though the balance sheet shows current liabilities exceeding current assets.

Sources of finance: When Gymshark needed capital to fund rapid growth, it sold a minority stake to General Atlantic (a private equity firm) in 2020, valuing the business at £1.3 billion. This provided significant capital without Ben Francis losing majority control — an example of how private equity can be used as an alternative to bank loans or issuing shares on a stock market.

Profit vs cash: Many fast-growing businesses have failed despite being profitable on paper. The construction industry is particularly prone to this — Carillion, a major UK construction company, was profitable in its accounts but collapsed in 2018 because it could not generate enough cash to meet its obligations. Profit on an income statement does not pay bills — cash does.

Gearing: Thames Water had gearing of over 80% before its financial difficulties became public. The heavy borrowing meant that a large proportion of its cash flow went to interest payments, leaving insufficient money for investment in infrastructure. This is an example of how excessive gearing can damage a business’s ability to invest and operate effectively.

Break-even in practice: A restaurant with high fixed costs (rent, staff salaries, equipment) needs to achieve a minimum level of covers per week to break even. If a new competitor opens nearby and takes 20% of customers, the margin of safety disappears. This shows why businesses with high fixed costs are particularly vulnerable to demand changes.

Tax avoidance: Starbucks generated over £400 million in UK sales in 2012 but paid no corporation tax, using legal mechanisms to shift profits to lower-tax countries. The resulting public backlash and boycotts led Starbucks to voluntarily pay £20 million in UK tax — demonstrating that while tax avoidance is legal, it can carry significant reputational and commercial costs.

Budget variance: During the COVID-19 pandemic, virtually every business experienced massive adverse revenue variances as lockdowns reduced demand. However, some businesses (online retailers, delivery services, technology companies) experienced favourable variances as demand surged. This illustrates how external events can make carefully prepared budgets immediately irrelevant.

ROI: In the SAM Paper 1, students calculate the ROI for Apple’s Vision Pro regional launch. Apple’s estimated operating profit of $330 million on a $3 billion investment gives an ROI of 11%. Whether this is “good” depends on Apple’s normal returns, the risk involved, and what alternative investments were available.

Evaluation 9. Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

Useful phrases for evaluation
“Whether this source of finance is suitable depends on the purpose and timescale of the investment — using short-term finance to fund a long-term project would create unnecessary risk.”

“The most important consideration is the trade-off between cost and control — loans must be repaid with interest but preserve ownership, while share capital dilutes control but requires no repayment.”

“This ratio needs to be interpreted in context — what is considered healthy varies significantly between industries, and a single snapshot does not show the trend over time.”

“While the current ratio suggests potential liquidity concerns, this ultimately depends on the speed of the cash cycle — a business that collects cash daily from customers may operate comfortably with a ratio below 1.”

“The key question is whether the increase in revenue exceeds the increase in costs — profit depends on the gap between the two, not on either figure in isolation.”

“On balance, the ROI of this investment looks acceptable, but the decision ultimately depends on the opportunity cost — what else could the business have done with this money, and would that have generated a higher return?”

“The budget variance is adverse, but whether this is a cause for concern depends on the reason behind it — an adverse cost variance caused by unexpectedly high demand may actually be positive for the business.”

“While high gearing increases financial risk, it also allows the business to grow without diluting ownership — the appropriateness depends on the stability of the business’s cash flow and the current cost of borrowing.”

Reminders 10. Exam Reminders

Things to remember
1. Profit ≠ cash. A business can be profitable but cash-poor. A business can have cash but be making a loss. Know the difference and use it in your answers.

2. Profit ≠ profitability. Profit is an absolute figure. Profitability is a ratio. Always check which one the question is asking about.

3. Payables = money you owe. Receivables = money owed to you. Get these the right way round.

4. Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit. Margin of safety = actual output − break-even output. Know both formulas and be able to use them quickly.

5. Current ratio and acid test ratio are not the same. The acid test strips out inventories because stock may not be easily converted to cash. A business with a healthy current ratio but a poor acid test may have too much money tied up in stock.

6. ROCE should exceed the cost of borrowing. If it does not, the business would have been better off putting its money in the bank. This is a useful benchmark for evaluation.

7. Gearing measures financial risk from debt. High gearing (> 50%) is not automatically bad — it depends on the stability of cash flow and the cost of borrowing. But it does increase vulnerability to interest rate rises and economic downturns.

8. Always show your working in calculations. The Own Figure Rule means you can get marks for correctly interpreting a wrong calculation, but only if the examiner can see your working.

9. Financial ratios are most useful in comparison — compare with previous years, competitors, and industry averages. A single ratio in isolation tells you very little.

10. Match the source of finance to the purpose. Short-term needs (working capital) should use short-term finance (overdraft, trade credit). Long-term investments should use long-term finance (loans, shares, retained profits).

11. SMART = Specific, Measurable, Accountable, Realistic, Time-specific. Not “Achievable”.

12. Ethics in finance is an A-level only topic. Tax avoidance is legal but may be unethical. Delayed payments to suppliers are legal but may harm small businesses. These tensions between profit and ethics are natural evaluation points.
