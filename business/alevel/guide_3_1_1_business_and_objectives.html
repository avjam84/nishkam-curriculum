3.1.1 2.1 Entrepreneurs

Why people start businesses

The spec asks you to understand both financial and non-financial reasons for setting up in business.

Financial reasons

Earning a profit, building personal wealth, creating a source of income, and (for some) the potential for very high returns if the business succeeds. Many entrepreneurs invest their own savings, so the financial return is tied directly to the risk they take.

Non-financial reasons

Being your own boss (independence), pursuing a passion or interest, wanting to make a difference (social purpose), flexibility in how and when you work, and the satisfaction of building something from nothing. For some entrepreneurs, these matter more than money — at least initially.

Example
Ben Francis (Gymshark) started making gym clothes in his parents’ garage while studying at university. He used his £5-per-hour Pizza Hut wages and £1,000 savings to buy a sewing machine. The initial motivation was passion for fitness and a gap in the market for affordable gymwear aimed at younger gym-goers — not a financial calculation.

Characteristics of entrepreneurs

The specification names six characteristics: risk taker, resilient, focused, passionate, innovative, adaptable. You need to be able to explain what each one means and, more importantly, why it matters for business success.

CharacteristicWhat it meansWhy it matters
Risk takerWilling to invest time and money without guarantee of successStarting a business always involves uncertainty. Without willingness to take calculated risks, opportunities are missed. But excessive risk-taking without planning can lead to failure.
ResilientAbility to recover from setbacks, rejection, or failureMost businesses face difficulties — failed products, cash flow crises, losing customers. Resilience allows the entrepreneur to learn from mistakes and keep going.
FocusedAbility to concentrate on goals and priorities without distractionEntrepreneurs face competing demands on their time. Staying focused on what matters most (e.g. product quality, customer needs) prevents the business from drifting.
PassionateGenuine enthusiasm for the product, service, or missionPassion sustains motivation through difficult periods and communicates authenticity to customers and employees. It is often what differentiates a start-up from established competitors.
InnovativeAbility to develop new ideas, products, or ways of doing thingsInnovation creates competitive advantage. An entrepreneur who can identify a gap in the market or find a better way to serve customers is more likely to succeed.
AdaptableWilling and able to change approach when circumstances changeMarkets, technology, and customer preferences change. An entrepreneur who clings to the original plan when it is no longer working will struggle. Adaptability means responding to feedback and external changes.
Common mistake
Students often list characteristics without explaining why they matter. In a 6-mark analyse question, you need to pick one characteristic and build a chain of reasoning showing how it contributed to a specific business outcome. Listing three characteristics with a sentence each will not score well.

Challenges of setting up in business

Starting a business is difficult. The main challenges include:

Raising finance — Banks may be reluctant to lend to unproven businesses. Personal savings may be limited. External investors may want a share of the business in return.

Building a customer base — New businesses have no reputation. Customers may be loyal to established competitors. Marketing budgets are usually small.

Cash flow — Revenue is often slow to come in, but costs (rent, stock, wages) start immediately. Many start-ups fail not because the idea is bad, but because they run out of cash before revenue catches up.

Competition — Established businesses have brand recognition, economies of scale, and existing customer relationships. A new entrant has to compete on all of these from a standing start.

Lack of experience — First-time entrepreneurs may have limited knowledge of financial management, employment law, marketing, or operations. Mistakes are common and can be costly.

The changing role of entrepreneurs as the business grows

This is a new area on this specification that was not prominent on the old spec. AQA expects you to understand that the entrepreneur’s role changes significantly as the business moves from start-up to established company.

StageEntrepreneur’s role
Start-upDoes everything — makes the product, sells to customers, manages the finances, answers the phone. Hands-on, operational, making quick decisions.
GrowthBegins to hire employees and delegate tasks. Needs to develop management skills — leading a team, setting objectives, building systems. The focus shifts from doing the work to managing people who do the work.
Established businessRole becomes more strategic — setting direction, making investment decisions, building partnerships, representing the brand. May appoint professional managers to run day-to-day operations. Some entrepreneurs struggle with this transition because they prefer hands-on involvement.
Example
Ben Francis (Gymshark) went from sewing gym clothes himself to leading a company valued at over $1 billion. He stepped back as CEO in 2017 to bring in professional management, but returned as CEO in 2021 because he felt the business had lost its direction. This illustrates both the need for the role to change and the tension that can arise when the entrepreneur’s vision conflicts with professional management.
Exam link
A question on the changing role of the entrepreneur could appear as a 6-mark analyse question (“Analyse one challenge an entrepreneur might face as their business grows”) or as part of a 9-mark assess question comparing the entrepreneur’s role at different stages.

3.1.1 2.2 Business Planning and Decisions

The business plan

Definition
A business plan is a document that sets out what a business intends to do and how it intends to do it. It typically includes the business idea, target market, marketing strategy, financial forecasts (revenue, costs, profit, cash flow), and sources of finance needed.

Purpose and value

A business plan serves several purposes: it forces the entrepreneur to think through the idea carefully before committing money; it identifies potential problems early (e.g. cash flow gaps); and it is usually required by banks and investors before they will provide finance. A plan also provides a benchmark to measure actual performance against.

However, the value of a business plan has limits. The market may change after the plan is written. Forecasts may turn out to be wrong. Some successful businesses (like Gymshark) grew organically without a formal plan. The plan is a guide, not a guarantee.

Competitiveness

Definition
Competitiveness is the ability of a business to compete effectively against its rivals. A competitive business offers something that customers value — whether that is lower prices, better quality, stronger branding, faster delivery, or superior customer service.

Competitiveness matters because businesses operate in markets with limited customers. A business that cannot compete will lose market share and eventually struggle to survive. Competitiveness runs through the entire specification — every functional area (marketing, finance, operations, people) contributes to it.

Influences on business decisions

The specification lists seven influences on business decisions. These are the factors that managers weigh up when making choices.

1. Objectives

What is the business trying to achieve? A decision that helps meet the objective is usually the right one. But objectives can conflict — a growth objective might require sacrificing short-term profit.

2. Risk

How likely is it that things could go wrong? Every business decision carries some risk. Launching a new product risks failure. Expanding into a new market risks losing money. The question is whether the potential reward justifies the risk.

3. Reward

What is the potential benefit? Higher potential rewards often come with higher risk. The entrepreneur must judge whether the trade-off is acceptable.

4. Resources

Does the business have the money, people, and equipment to carry out the decision? A business with limited resources may have to choose between competing priorities.

5. Market conditions

Is the market growing or shrinking? What are competitors doing? Is there strong demand for the product? Decisions made in a booming market look different from those made during a recession.

6. Ethics

Is this the right thing to do? Ethical considerations include how the decision affects employees, the environment, customers, and the community. Doing the ethical thing may cost more in the short term but protect the brand in the long term.

7. Opportunity costs

Definition
Opportunity cost is the next best alternative that is given up when a decision is made. If a business spends £500,000 on a new marketing campaign, the opportunity cost is whatever else that £500,000 could have been used for — perhaps hiring new staff, or investing in product development.

Opportunity cost is important because resources are limited. Every decision involves a trade-off. AQA wants students to recognise that choosing one option means losing the benefits of the alternative. This is a useful evaluation point — even if a decision looks good, the opportunity cost might be significant.

Common mistake
Students often treat opportunity cost as just “the cost of something.” It is specifically the next best alternative forgone. If a question asks about opportunity cost, you must identify what was given up, not just what was spent.

Importance of risks and rewards

Risk and reward are central to business decision-making. Entrepreneurs take risks because the potential rewards justify them. But the relationship is not always straightforward:

• A low-risk, low-reward decision might be appropriate for a business focused on survival.

• A high-risk, high-reward decision might be appropriate for a business trying to grow rapidly or enter a new market.

• The same decision might be high-risk for one business and low-risk for another — it depends on resources, experience, and market position.

Ethical dilemmas in decision-making

An ethical dilemma arises when doing the right thing conflicts with doing the profitable thing. The specification specifically mentions profit vs ethics.

Examples of ethical dilemmas
Sourcing: A clothing manufacturer could source fabrics from a low-cost supplier in a country with poor labour conditions. This increases profit but raises ethical concerns about worker exploitation.

Environment: A manufacturer could reduce costs by using cheaper packaging that is not recyclable. This improves profit margins but harms the environment.

Pricing: A pharmaceutical company could charge very high prices for a life-saving medicine because demand is inelastic. This maximises profit but limits access to treatment.

In each case, the business must weigh short-term profit against long-term brand reputation, customer loyalty, and the wider impact on society.
Evaluation point
Whether a business prioritises profit or ethics often depends on its ownership and objectives. A social enterprise will almost always prioritise ethics. A PLC under pressure from shareholders to deliver quarterly returns may struggle to justify ethical decisions that reduce short-term profit, even if they are better for the business in the long run. This is a useful line of argument in 9-mark and 15-mark questions.

3.1.1 2.3 Business Objectives

Purpose and value of setting objectives

Objectives give a business direction. Without them, decisions are made reactively rather than strategically. Objectives also allow the business to measure performance — if the objective was to increase sales by 10%, the business can check at the end of the year whether it achieved this.

Objectives help with coordination — when everyone in the business knows what they are working towards, departments can align their efforts. They also help with motivation — employees who understand the goal and can see their contribution to it are more likely to be engaged.

SMART objectives

Key terms — AQA wording
The specification states that effective objectives should be:

Specific — clearly defined, not vague
Measurable — you can tell whether it has been achieved
Accountable — someone is responsible for achieving it
Realistic — achievable given the resources available
Time specific — has a deadline
Important
The AQA specification uses “Accountable” not “Achievable” for the A in SMART. Many textbooks use “Achievable” but the spec is clear. Use “Accountable” in your answers — it means someone is responsible for making sure the objective is met.

Why SMART objectives are useful

A vague objective like “increase profit” gives no clear target. “Increase operating profit by 8% within the next financial year, with the marketing director accountable” is specific, measurable, has someone responsible, is realistic (assuming it is based on evidence), and has a deadline. It is much easier to plan towards, track progress on, and evaluate.

Limitations

Not everything that matters can easily be made SMART. Objectives like “improve our reputation” or “build a positive culture” are important but harder to measure precisely. There is also a risk that focusing too narrowly on measurable targets leads businesses to ignore things that matter but are harder to quantify.

Example
A school setting an objective of “90% of students achieve grade 4+ in English GCSE” is SMART. But if teachers focus only on this target, they might neglect students who are already above grade 4 or reduce time spent on subjects that are not being measured. The same tension exists in business — what gets measured gets managed, but not everything worth managing gets measured.

Traps 4. Common Mistakes

1. Listing characteristics instead of analysing one
In a 6-mark analyse question on entrepreneurial characteristics, pick one and develop a chain of reasoning. Don’t list three with a sentence each — that is description, not analysis.
2. Confusing opportunity cost with just “cost”
Opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up. It is not the price paid. If a question mentions opportunity cost, identify what was sacrificed, not what was spent.
3. Writing “Achievable” instead of “Accountable”
The AQA spec uses “Accountable” for the A in SMART. Many GCSE textbooks use “Achievable”. Use the AQA wording.
4. Treating ethical dilemmas as one-sided
An ethical dilemma means there are genuine arguments on both sides. “The business should always be ethical” is too simple. In an assess or evaluate question, explore the trade-off between short-term profit and long-term reputation.
5. Ignoring the changing role of the entrepreneur
This is new to the spec. If a question asks about an entrepreneur in a growing business, consider whether their role needs to change — and what happens if they resist that change.

6-mark Analyse: “Analyse how one entrepreneurial characteristic has contributed to the success of Gymshark Ltd.” [6 marks] — SAM Paper 1 Q1.1

Show model answer

One characteristic of an entrepreneur is being innovative — the ability to develop new ideas or find better ways of doing things. Ben Francis identified a gap in the market for affordable gymwear aimed at younger gym-goers and, rather than selling through traditional retail, built Gymshark’s brand almost entirely through social media influencers on Instagram and TikTok. This meant that Gymshark avoided the high costs of renting retail space on the high street, which allowed the business to keep prices competitive against established rivals like Nike and Adidas while still maintaining healthy profit margins. This is an example of market mapping — Ben identified a gap where customer needs were not being met by existing competitors and positioned Gymshark to fill it. As a result, Gymshark was able to attract 18 million social media followers, giving it direct access to its target customers without relying on expensive advertising campaigns. This could have contributed to the rapid growth that led to the business being valued at over $1 billion by 2021, which shows that Ben’s innovative approach to distribution and marketing gave Gymshark a competitive advantage that would not have been possible through traditional methods alone.

What makes this work
One characteristic, clearly identified. Applied to the business throughout — not generic. A chain of reasoning with connectives (“this meant that”, “which allowed”, “as a result”, “which shows that”). The chain has a clear start point (innovation) and end point (competitive advantage and growth).

9-mark Assess: “Assess whether increasing spending on influencers would lead to higher profits for Gymshark Ltd.” [9 marks] — SAM Paper 1 Q1.3

Structure — FOR → AGAINST → DECIDE
Each argument paragraph needs: case study data including manipulated data woven throughout → at least five chains of analysis using connectives (this means that, as a result, therefore, this could lead to, this is because) reaching the endpoint → business models or theories applied where relevant.

The weighted judgement follows a fixed formula: Overall… → Although… carries greater weight because… → However, this ultimately depends on… → Therefore [final sentence answering the question in the words of the question].

Step 1 — Argument FOR (increasing spending on influencers would lead to higher profits)

Show model answer: Argument for

On one hand, one could argue that increasing spending on influencers would lead to higher profits for Gymshark Ltd. Gymshark already has 18 million social media followers and built its entire brand through influencer marketing on Instagram, TikTok and Facebook rather than traditional advertising or high street retail. This means that the business has a proven model for converting influencer exposure into sales, and therefore increasing investment in this area would be building on an approach that has already been shown to work rather than experimenting with an unproven strategy. This is because Gymshark’s target market of younger gym-goers — particularly the 18–24 age group, which makes up the highest proportion of TikTok’s 1.6 billion monthly users — is far more likely to discover and trust a brand through social media influencers than through TV advertising or billboards. As a result, each additional pound spent on influencer partnerships could reach a highly engaged audience at a lower cost per customer than traditional marketing channels, which could lead to increased brand awareness and higher sales volumes without the fixed cost burden of renting retail space. In terms of the Ansoff Matrix, this would represent a market penetration strategy — selling more of the same product to the same type of customer through increased promotion, which is generally lower risk than diversification or new market development. This could further lead to improved profit margins because the variable nature of influencer deals — typically paid per post or commission — means marketing costs scale with output rather than being committed in advance, unlike a lease on a Regent Street store. This shows that increasing influencer spending could lead to higher profits for Gymshark because the cost of acquiring each new customer is likely to remain lower than through competing marketing channels.

Step 2 — Argument AGAINST (increasing spending on influencers would not lead to higher profits)

Show model answer: Argument against

On the other hand, one might argue that increasing spending on influencers would not lead to higher profits for Gymshark Ltd. Gymshark competes against Nike, Adidas and Puma — brands with marketing budgets that dwarf Gymshark’s. Nike’s brand alone was worth $31 billion in 2023, and Ben Francis has said he wants Gymshark to become the UK equivalent of Nike. This means that simply spending more on influencers may not be enough to close the gap with competitors who have decades of brand equity, global distribution networks and vast resources for sponsorship and product development. Using Porter’s Five Forces, the competitive rivalry in the sportswear market is intense, and the bargaining power of customers is high because they can easily switch between brands — which limits how much any single marketing strategy can shift market share. As a result, the additional influencer spending could increase Gymshark’s marketing costs without generating a proportionate increase in revenue, therefore compressing profit margins rather than improving them. This is because the sportswear market is highly competitive, and market research suggests that 43% of people who buy Nike sportswear would buy it again — meaning strong brand loyalty to established rivals limits how many new customers Gymshark can attract regardless of influencer spend. This could lead to a situation where Gymshark is spending more to fight for the same pool of customers, with each additional influencer generating fewer incremental sales. There is also an opportunity cost to consider — every pound spent on influencers is a pound not spent on product development, international expansion, or improving supply chain efficiency, any of which might generate a higher return. This shows that increasing influencer spending would not necessarily lead to higher profits for Gymshark because the additional revenue generated may not exceed the additional cost, particularly in a market dominated by well-established competitors.

Step 3 — Weighted judgement

Show model answer: Weighted judgement

Overall, increasing spending on influencers could lead to higher profits for Gymshark Ltd, but only if the additional investment is targeted at new markets and audiences rather than saturating existing ones. Although the competitive threat from Nike, Adidas and Puma is real, and the risk of diminishing returns is significant, the argument for increasing influencer spending carries greater weight because Gymshark’s entire business model has been built on influencer marketing rather than traditional retail — the company grew from a garage to a $1 billion valuation without high street stores or TV advertising, proving that this approach works for its target market. The fact that 43% of Nike buyers are loyal does not mean the remaining 57% are unreachable, and Gymshark’s 18 million followers demonstrate it has already captured significant attention among younger consumers. However, this ultimately depends on where the additional spending is directed — if it is concentrated on TikTok influencers in Asia and North America, where Gymshark is still growing, the return on investment is likely to be significantly higher than if it is spent on UK influencers where the brand is already well established. Therefore, increasing spending on influencers would lead to higher profits for Gymshark Ltd provided the investment is directed at new markets with high growth potential, because the proven effectiveness of Gymshark’s influencer model means that well-targeted additional spending is likely to generate revenue that exceeds the cost.

Why this is Level 3 (7–9 marks)
AO1: Knowledge of influencer marketing, brand loyalty, profit margins, diminishing returns — shown through the argument, not separate definitions.
AO2: Data used and manipulated throughout: 18 million followers, $31 billion Nike brand, 43% repurchase rate (and the implied 57% not locked in), 1.6 billion TikTok users, $1 billion valuation, Regent Street store, Asia and North America expansion plans.
AO3: Both paragraphs have at least five chains of analysis using connectives (this means that, therefore, as a result, this could lead to, this is because, this shows that). Both chains reach the endpoint — whether influencer spending leads to higher profits.
AO4: The judgement commits to a position, acknowledges the counterargument, explains why one side carries more weight, adds a condition (“depends on where the spending is directed”), and the final sentence answers the question using the words of the question.
Sophisticated concepts: The Ansoff Matrix (market penetration), Porter’s Five Forces (competitive rivalry, buyer power), opportunity cost, and market mapping are all woven into the analysis naturally — they strengthen the argument without being forced in.

Concepts 7. Sophisticated Concepts

The key is relevance — apply them where they genuinely add to the argument, not just to show you know them. What is not on the new spec
If you have seen old textbooks or resources, note that the following models are not on the new AQA 7138 specification: Handy’s cultural typology, Elkington’s Triple Bottom Line (replaced by the simpler “Triple Bottom Line” without Elkington’s name), Porter’s generic strategies, Kaplan and Norton’s Balanced Scorecard, and Bartlett and Ghoshal. Do not use these in your answers.
How to use them in your answers
You do not need to define a model in full before using it. The best approach is to weave it into your analysis naturally. For example, instead of writing “The Ansoff Matrix is a model that shows four growth strategies...” and then applying it, simply write: “In terms of the Ansoff Matrix, this would represent a market penetration strategy, which is generally lower risk than diversification.” The pink highlighting in the model answers above shows you where sophisticated concepts have been applied.

Examples 8. Real-World Examples

Real-world examples
Entrepreneur — passion and risk: Ben Francis (Gymshark) used his Pizza Hut wages and taught himself to sew. Dropped out of university to focus on the business full-time. The financial risk was significant for a 19-year-old, but the passion for fitness and the gap in the market drove the decision.

Entrepreneur — changing role: Sara Blakely (Spanx) started by selling her product from her apartment. As the business grew to $1 billion+ revenue, she brought in professional management but retained creative control. The transition from doing everything to leading strategically is a common challenge.

Ethical dilemma — profit vs ethics: Primark has faced criticism for using suppliers in countries with low labour standards. The low prices that customers value depend partly on low production costs. Switching to fully ethical suppliers would increase costs and potentially prices — risking the competitive advantage that built the brand.

Opportunity cost: When Apple spent over $8 billion developing Vision Pro, the opportunity cost was enormous — that money could have been used for other product development, share buybacks, or higher dividends. Whether the investment was worthwhile depends on Vision Pro’s long-term commercial success.

SMART objectives: Gymshark’s objective of becoming “the UK equivalent of Nike” is aspirational but not SMART — it is not time-specific or easily measurable. A SMART version might be: “Achieve £1 billion annual revenue by 2028, with the CEO accountable.”

Business plan — limitations: Gymshark grew organically without a traditional business plan, relying instead on social media experimentation and rapid response to what worked. This challenges the assumption that a formal plan is always essential.

Evaluation 9. Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

Useful phrases for evaluation
“This depends on the stage of the business — a start-up may need to prioritise survival over ethics, while an established business can afford to take a longer-term view.”

“The most significant factor is arguably the opportunity cost of the decision, because even a good decision has a cost if it means a better alternative was not pursued.”

“Whether this characteristic is the most important depends on the market the business operates in — innovation matters more in technology than in commodities, for example.”

“The extent to which objectives drive performance depends on whether they are genuinely SMART or simply aspirational statements that no one is held accountable for.”

“On balance, the ethical argument carries greater weight for businesses whose brand depends on trust, but less weight for businesses competing primarily on price.”

“Ultimately, the effectiveness of a business plan depends on how flexible the entrepreneur is in adapting it when market conditions change.”

Reminders 10. Exam Reminders

Things to remember
1. The six entrepreneurial characteristics are: risk taker, resilient, focused, passionate, innovative, adaptable. Know what each means and be able to apply them to a case study business.

2. The entrepreneur’s role changes as the business grows. At start-up they do everything; as the business grows they must delegate and become more strategic. Not all entrepreneurs manage this transition well.

3. SMART = Specific, Measurable, Accountable, Realistic, Time specific. Not “Achievable”.

4. Opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up — not just the cost of something. Use it as an evaluation point: “even if this decision is good, the opportunity cost is...”

5. Ethical dilemmas involve a genuine trade-off between doing the right thing and doing the profitable thing. Don’t be one-sided — explore both sides and make a judgement that depends on context.

6. Business plans are useful but not infallible. They help with raising finance and thinking through the idea, but markets change and forecasts can be wrong.

7. Competitiveness runs through the entire specification. It is not just a standalone topic — every decision a business makes either strengthens or weakens its competitive position.

8. In 6-mark analyse questions, pick one point and develop it with a chain of reasoning. Use connectives: “this means that”, “as a result”, “which could lead to”, “this shows that”.

9. In 9-mark assess questions, use: FOR → AGAINST → weighted judgement. The judgement must come down on one side with a reason, not just “it depends.”

10. Apply the case study throughout your answer. Use names, figures, and details from the stimulus material. Generic answers cannot access the top marks.
