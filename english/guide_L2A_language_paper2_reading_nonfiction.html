Concepts

Content

L2A.1 Summarising

Key definitionSummary: synthesising evidence from two texts to explain differences or similarities on a given focus.

Make inferences, not just retrieve. Use comparative connectives (“whereas”, “similarly”).

ExampleTwo texts about schools: one describes Victorian discipline, the other modern classrooms; the summary infers how attitudes to children have changed.
Common mistakeWriting about language methods in a summary question.
Evaluation pointInference is the key: say what the evidence implies.
Exam linkA summary question focuses on both sources.

L2A.2 Comparing viewpoints

Key definitionViewpoint: a writer’s attitude or perspective on a topic.

Compare the writers’ perspectives AND the methods they use to convey them, moving between texts throughout.

ExampleOne writer is enthusiastic about a journey, the other fearful; compare their tone and imagery.
Common mistakeWriting about each text separately with no comparison.
Evaluation pointThe best answers compare methods and attitudes together.
Exam linkA comparison question asks you to compare how writers convey their perspectives.

L2A.3 Language analysis in non-fiction

Key definitionNon-fiction language analysis: explaining how a writer’s word choices shape the reader’s view of a topic.

One question on Paper 2 Section A asks how a writer uses language in one source. The skills are the same as Paper 1, but the purpose differs: non-fiction writers usually aim to inform, persuade or share a viewpoint. Always connect the language to the writer’s attitude.

Useful methods in non-fiction include direct address, emotive language, hyperbole, statistics, anecdote, rhetorical questions and tone shifts. Name a method only when it helps your explanation.

ExampleA writer describing a crowded workhouse as a “cage” reveals a critical attitude to how the poor are treated.
Common mistakeIgnoring the writer’s purpose turns analysis into description.
Evaluation pointAlways ask: what does this word reveal about the writer’s viewpoint?
Exam linkThis question usually focuses on one of the two sources [to confirm which].

L2A.4 Reading older sources

Key definition19th-century non-fiction: older letters, diaries, journalism and speeches, often with longer sentences and formal vocabulary.

Paper 2 pairs a modern source with an older one. Older texts can feel dense; read them twice. Identify the topic, the writer’s attitude and any differences from modern attitudes. The map notes students’ prior 19th-century context work, which helps here.

ExampleA Victorian letter about a child labourer may treat the child’s work as normal, whereas a modern article treats child labour as abuse.
Common mistakeAssuming the older writer shares modern values.
Evaluation pointContrasting historical attitudes is often the key to the comparison question.
Exam linkBoth sources are needed for the summary and comparison questions.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Retrieving without inferring in the summary. Why it is wrong: The summary rewards what evidence implies, not just what it says. Instead: Follow every quotation with “this suggests…”.
  2. Writing about each source separately in comparison. Why it is wrong: The question asks you to compare; separate essays lose the comparative mark. Instead: Move between sources in every paragraph using comparative connectives.
  3. Analysing language in the summary question. Why it is wrong: Methods are not assessed there and waste time. Instead: Focus on content and inference.
  4. Assuming the older writer shares modern values. Why it is wrong: This leads to misreading the attitude. Instead: Consider historical difference explicitly.
  5. Spending too long on low-tariff questions. Why it is wrong: High-tariff comparison time is lost. Instead: Follow a time plan [timings to confirm].

Answers

Colour-Coded Model Answers

AO1 Identify / interpret / synthesiseAO2 Language & structure / methodsAO3 Compare viewpoints & methodsAO4 Evaluate critically / AO5–6 writing [AO labels to be confirmed by teacher]Connective

Summary / synthesis: Write a summary of the differences between the two schools.[tariff to confirm]

In Source A, pupils “stood in silence for hours”, which suggests Victorian schools valued obedience. Whereas in Source B pupils “debate freely”, implying modern schools value independent thinking.

What makes this workSynthesises both sources with inference and comparative connectives.
Analytical — point / evidence / analysis / context: How does the writer use language to describe the workhouse?[tariff to confirm]

POINT: The writer is critical of the workhouse. EVIDENCE: it is a “cage”. ANALYSIS: the metaphor presents residents as trapped animals; this suggests they are denied dignity, so the reader feels outrage.

What makes this workPoint-evidence-analysis chain linked to attitude.
Comparative: Compare how the writers convey their perspectives on travel.[tariff to confirm]

Whereas Writer A presents travel as liberating, Writer B presents it as dangerous. Writer A “flew” across the countryside; the verb conveys exhilaration. In contrast, Writer B’s “trapped” carriage conveys fear. Both use first-person anecdote, yet to opposite effect.

Context points in this answer: [to be confirmed by teacher]

What makes this workCompares attitudes AND methods throughout.
Examples

Real-World & Context Examples

  • Year 9 ‘Protest’ anthology. The map shows non-fiction on racial inequality, food poverty and masculinity. These modern texts mirror the style of Paper 2 modern sources. They are full of viewpoint and persuasive language. Re-reading them builds speed in identifying attitude.
  • Victorian journalism. 19th-century writers exposed social conditions in newspapers and letters. Their attitudes to poverty and class differ from modern views. Recognising this difference is central to the comparison question. It links to the 19th-century context students already know. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Newspaper opinion columns. Columnists state a clear viewpoint and support it with anecdote and rhetoric. Reading one daily is excellent practice for spotting perspective. Ask what the writer wants you to think. Then ask how they make you think it. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Charity appeals. Appeals use emotive language, statistics and direct address. They are short, purposeful non-fiction ideal for analysis practice. Their persuasive methods recur in exam sources. They also model writing for Section B. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Travel writing old and new. Victorian travel accounts and modern blogs often describe similar journeys with different attitudes. This makes them ideal comparison practice. Notice changes in what writers find dangerous or exciting. Such shifts often form the basis of exam comparisons. [to be confirmed by teacher]
Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • Both writers are concerned with childhood, but their attitudes differ sharply.
  • Whereas Writer A is optimistic, Writer B is fearful, and this contrast is clear in their imagery.
  • The older writer’s acceptance of hardship reflects attitudes of the time, which a modern reader may find troubling.
  • Similarly, both writers use anecdote, yet one uses it to inspire and the other to warn.
  • Ultimately, the writers differ because their historical contexts shape what they consider normal.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Read the older source twice.
  2. Infer, don’t just retrieve, in the summary.
  3. Connect language to the writer’s attitude.
  4. Compare attitudes and methods in every paragraph.
  5. Use comparative connectives.
