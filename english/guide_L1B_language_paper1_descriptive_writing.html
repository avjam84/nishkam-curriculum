Concepts

Content

L1B.1 Crafting description

Key definitionDescriptive writing: writing that creates a vivid picture of a place, person or moment through sensory detail and controlled structure.

Plan a structure (e.g. a cyclical description, or a shift from wide to close focus). Use precise vocabulary, varied sentence openings and the five senses. Show, don’t tell.

ExampleInstead of “the market was busy”, write “Voices tangled above the stalls; a crate of mangoes split open, sweetening the air.”
Common mistakeWriting a long plot with no description, or piling up adjectives.
Evaluation pointRestraint is more powerful than excess: one precise image beats three adjectives.
Exam linkSection B rewards content and organisation and, separately, technical accuracy.

L1B.2 Technical accuracy (SPaG)

Key definitionTechnical accuracy: correct spelling, punctuation and grammar plus a range of sentence structures.

Use a range of punctuation (semicolons, colons, dashes) accurately; vary sentence length for effect; proofread in the last minutes.

ExampleA one-word sentence — “Silence.” — after a long sentence creates impact.
Common mistakeComma splices (“It was cold, I shivered”).
Evaluation pointAccuracy is a separately weighted mark, so proofreading time is never wasted.
Exam linkTechnical accuracy is assessed on every Section B response.

L1B.3 Planning and structuring a description

Key definitionStructural planning: deciding the order of focus before writing so the piece has shape and an ending.

Strong descriptive writing is planned around a structure, not a list of images. Choose one model and commit to it.

StructureHow it worksEffect
CyclicalEnd by returning to the opening image, changedA sense of completion and reflection
ZoomWide setting → small group → single detailGuides the reader’s attention like a camera
Time shiftDawn → midday → dusk, or present → memory → presentShows change and contrast
ContrastNoise → silence, crowd → solitudeCreates impact at the turning point

Spend about five minutes planning five paragraphs. Write a one-line focus for each, plus one ambitious image you will use. This prevents the common problem of running out of ideas or writing an endless middle with no ending.

ExamplePlan for a market: (1) wide view of stalls at dawn; (2) a single trader setting out fruit; (3) noise peaks at midday; (4) sudden silence as rain begins; (5) the empty market at dusk, echoing paragraph 1.
Common mistakeBeginning without a plan usually produces a piece with no ending, which weakens the organisation mark.
Evaluation pointA planned structural shift is one of the clearest signals of a crafted response.
Exam linkContent and organisation are assessed together in Section B [weighting to confirm].

L1B.4 Narrative writing

Key definitionNarrative: a story with a controlled plot, characters and a resolution or deliberate open ending.

Some Section B tasks allow a narrative. The biggest risk is an over-ambitious plot. Keep to one main character, one setting and one key moment of change.

Use a limited time span (one hour, one morning) so that you can describe in depth. Begin in the middle of the action or with a striking image. Build tension with withheld information, then resolve or leave a deliberate cliffhanger. Dialogue should be brief and set out correctly on new lines.

ExampleA story about a student waiting for exam results can be told entirely within the five minutes before the envelope is opened.
Common mistakeStories that cover years of events in two pages become summaries, not crafted writing.
Evaluation pointDepth over breadth: one moment, richly told, outperforms a sprawling plot.
Exam linkThe task may offer a picture or a story opening as stimulus [format to confirm].

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Not planning. Why it is wrong: Unplanned writing usually lacks a clear structure and ending. Instead: Spend five minutes planning paragraphs and a structural shift.
  2. Comma splices (“It was cold, I shivered”). Why it is wrong: Joining two sentences with a comma is a grammatical error that lowers technical accuracy. Instead: Use a full stop, semicolon or conjunction.
  3. Over-long narrative plots. Why it is wrong: Covering many events leaves no room for descriptive detail. Instead: Limit the story to one moment and one setting.
  4. Piling up adjectives. Why it is wrong: Several adjectives dilute impact and read as unselective. Instead: Choose one precise noun or verb instead.
  5. Ignoring the picture or prompt. Why it is wrong: The task asks for a response to that stimulus; drifting from it weakens content. Instead: Keep returning to details from the picture.
  6. No time to proofread. Why it is wrong: Careless errors cost technical accuracy marks that are easy to secure. Instead: Reserve the final minutes for checking.

Extended writing: Write a description of a busy market as suggested by the picture.[tariff to confirm]

Voices tangled above the stalls; a crate of mangoes split open, sweetening the air. A trader barked prices, hoarse and hopeful. Then — silence. The first drops of rain freckled the canvas, and a single coin rolled, unchased, across the cobbles.

What makes this workSensory detail, a semicolon and dash used accurately, a deliberate shift to silence and a precise final image show crafted writing.
Extended writing: Write the opening of a story about waiting.[tariff to confirm]

The envelope sat on the table like a held breath. Amrit did not touch it. Outside, a bus sighed at the stop and pulled away without her.

What makes this workStarts in the moment, uses simile and personification, and limits the time span so detail can grow.
Examples

Real-World & Context Examples

  • Published novel openings. Many novels begin with a single striking image before introducing a character. Studying how authors do this shows you that description and story can work together. Copy the structure, not the words. This gives you a reliable opening move in the exam. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Year 9 Protest speeches. The map shows students crafting speeches using rhetorical devices. Although descriptive writing is not persuasive, the same control of rhythm and sentence length applies. A short sentence after a long one works in both forms. Practising one strengthens the other.
  • Photography. Section B may offer a picture as stimulus. Photographers frame a scene and choose a focal point. Asking ‘what is the focal point of this picture?’ gives you your zoom-in moment. Describing what lies outside the frame adds originality.
  • Travel writing. Travel journalism uses sensory description to place readers somewhere they have never been. It is a real-world model for descriptive writing that is vivid but controlled. Notice how travel writers use one striking detail per paragraph. This is exactly the restraint that examiners reward.
  • Redrafting. The map highlights feedback and re-writing as central to the Nishkam English approach. Professional writers redraft many times. Practising a paragraph, then rewriting it with stronger verbs and varied sentences, is the most efficient way to improve. Use the Redraft Tool for this.
Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • (This is a writing question, so instead of evaluation phrases use these crafted structural moves.)
  • Return to your opening image at the end, but show how it has changed.
  • Move from a wide view of the setting to a single, telling detail.
  • Place a one-word sentence at the turning point to create impact.
  • Shift the time of day to show change across the description.
  • End on a quiet, precise image rather than a dramatic event.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Plan for about five minutes.
  2. Choose one structure and commit to it.
  3. Use the senses, but select carefully.
  4. Vary sentence openings and lengths.
  5. Use a range of punctuation accurately.
  6. Proofread in the final minutes.
