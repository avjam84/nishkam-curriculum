Concepts

Content

L1A.1 Analysing language

Key definitionLanguage analysis: explaining how a writer’s word choices, imagery and sentence forms create effects on the reader.

Select a short quotation, name the method only if it helps, then zoom in on a single word and explain its connotations and effect. Link every point back to the focus of the question.

ExampleIn a description of a storm, “the sky bled” uses a verb that personifies the sky as wounded, suggesting violence and pain rather than simple weather.
Common mistakeFeature-spotting: naming “a simile” without explaining what it makes the reader think or feel earns little credit.
Evaluation pointThe most effective analysis explores more than one possible connotation of the same word.
Exam linkQuestions ask how the writer uses language to describe a specified focus in a named extract of lines.

L1A.2 Analysing structure

Key definitionStructure: how a text is organised at whole-text, paragraph and sentence level — openings, shifts in focus, endings, repetition and contrast.

Track the reader’s journey: what are we shown first, where does the focus shift (outside → inside, wide → narrow, calm → tension), and why? Talk about the text as a constructed whole, not line-by-line language.

ExampleAn extract that opens with a wide view of a town and narrows to one figure at a window creates intrigue by directing attention to a character.
Common mistakeAnalysing individual words in a structure question; structure is about order and focus.
Evaluation pointA shift in focus is most powerful when it changes the reader’s expectations.
Exam linkA structure question asks how the writer has structured the text to interest the reader.

L1A.3 Evaluating a statement

Key definitionEvaluation: judging how far you agree with a statement about a text, supported by analysis of methods.

Agree or disagree clearly, then prove your view with references and analysis of how the writer achieves the effect named in the statement.

ExampleStatement: “The writer makes the setting feel threatening.” A strong answer weighs the menacing imagery against moments of calm that heighten later tension.
Common mistakeRetelling the story instead of judging how successfully the writer creates the effect.
Evaluation pointUse evaluative language: “convincingly”, “most strikingly”, “to some extent”.
Exam linkThe evaluation question is usually the highest-tariff reading question in Section A.

L1A.4 Retrieving explicit information

Key definitionExplicit information: facts stated directly in the text, as opposed to ideas that must be inferred.

The first reading task asks you to locate clear facts in a specified set of lines. Everything you write must be taken from those lines only, and it must be true according to the text. This is a test of careful reading, not interpretation.

Work systematically: underline the line range, then scan for concrete details about the named focus (a place, a person, an object). Short phrases or your own brief paraphrases are both acceptable, but each point must be a separate fact.

StepWhat to doWhy
1Box the line numbersFacts from outside the lines earn nothing
2Re-read the focus in the questionA true fact about the wrong subject earns nothing
3Write one fact per pointCombining facts can cost marks
4Move on quicklyTime is better spent on higher-tariff questions

ExampleIf the question asks for things you learn about the house, “the windows were cracked” is explicit; “the owners were poor” is an inference and should not be used here.
Common mistakeWriting inferences or analysis in a retrieval question wastes time and can make the point incorrect.
Evaluation pointAccuracy matters more than elegance here; precise, separate facts are what is rewarded.
Exam linkThis is normally the first, lowest-tariff question on Paper 1 [tariff to confirm].

L1A.5 Building a whole-text reading of an unseen extract

Key definitionReading for gist: forming an overall understanding of what happens, who is involved and what mood is created before analysing detail.

The map states that students analyse language and structure “to a range of unseen texts”. Because you have never seen the extract, your first reading should establish: who is the focus, where are we, what changes between the opening and the ending, and what the dominant mood is.

A useful annotation habit is to mark the extract in three zones — beginning, middle, end — and write a one-word mood label for each (e.g. calm → uneasy → frightened). This gives you instant material for the structure question and helps you choose relevant quotations for the evaluation question.

Look out for the writer’s choices of narrative voice (first or third person), tense, and whether we are given a character’s thoughts. These shape how close the reader feels to the action and are valid points in both language and structure answers.

ExampleA third-person extract that suddenly moves into a character’s thoughts (“She knew, then, that nobody was coming.”) shifts the reader from observer to participant.
Common mistakeStarting to annotate language before understanding the extract leads to misreadings.
Evaluation pointAn overview of change across the extract strengthens every later answer.
Exam linkGist reading feeds the structure and evaluation questions directly.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Quoting long chunks of the extract. Why it is wrong: Long quotations hide which word you are analysing and the examiner cannot tell what you have understood. Instead: Select two to five words and zoom in on the single most powerful one.
  2. Writing about lines outside the given range. Why it is wrong: The question tests your reading of a specific section; points outside it gain no credit. Instead: Box the line numbers before you start reading.
  3. Feature-spotting (“the writer uses a simile”). Why it is wrong: Naming a device shows recognition, not analysis; marks reward explanation of effect. Instead: Explain what the image makes the reader think or feel and why.
  4. Treating the structure question like a language question. Why it is wrong: Structure is about order, focus and shifts across the text; word analysis misses the target. Instead: Track beginning, middle and end and explain why the focus moves.
  5. Giving no judgement in the evaluation question. Why it is wrong: The question asks to what extent you agree; without a view the response cannot reach the higher levels. Instead: State your view at the start and return to it at the end.
  6. Spending too long on early low-tariff questions. Why it is wrong: Time lost early cannot be recovered on the high-tariff evaluation question. Instead: Keep to a time plan [timings to confirm].

Answers

Colour-Coded Model Answers

AO1 Identify / interpret / synthesiseAO2 Language & structure / methodsAO3 Compare viewpoints & methodsAO4 Evaluate critically / AO5–6 writing [AO labels to be confirmed by teacher]Connective

Retrieval: List four things you learn about the house from these lines.[tariff to confirm]

1. The windows were cracked. 2. The door hung from one hinge. 3. Ivy covered the walls. 4. The garden was overgrown.

What makes this workFour separate, explicit facts, all from the given lines, with no inference.
Analytical — point / evidence / analysis / context: How does the writer use language to describe the storm?[tariff to confirm]

POINT: The writer presents the storm as violent and threatening. EVIDENCE: “the sky bled”. ANALYSIS: The verb “bled” personifies the sky as a wounded body; this suggests that the storm is causing injury, and the red connotations of blood foreshadow danger. As a result, the reader feels uneasy rather than impressed by the weather.

What makes this workPrecise selection, a single-word zoom, connotations explored, and the effect on the reader explained.
Structure analysis: How has the writer structured the text to interest you?[tariff to confirm]

At the beginning, the writer opens with a wide view of the town at dusk, which establishes a calm setting. In the middle, the focus narrows to a single lit window, which creates intrigue as we wonder who is inside. At the end, the narrative shifts into the character’s thoughts, so that the reader shares her fear directly.

What makes this workCovers the whole text, names structural shifts and explains why each interests the reader.
Evaluative — FOR / AGAINST / weighted judgement: “In this part of the story the writer makes the setting feel threatening.” To what extent do you agree?[tariff to confirm]

VIEW: I strongly agree that the setting feels threatening. FOR: the image of trees that “clawed” at the windows makes nature seem predatory, which means the house feels under attack. AGAINST: however, the opening description of “soft lamplight” is comforting, yet this calm only heightens the later menace. WEIGHTED JUDGEMENT: therefore, the brief warmth is a deliberate contrast, and overall the threat dominates.

What makes this workA clear view, evidence for and against, and a weighted judgement that explains how the counter-evidence actually supports the overall reading.
Examples

Real-World & Context Examples

  • Opening chapters of novels. Unseen Paper 1 extracts are typically taken from 20th- and 21st-century fiction. Reading the first chapter of any well-known novel and asking how the writer hooks the reader is direct practice. Notice how many openings establish setting before introducing a character. You can reuse this observation in the structure question. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Year 9 ‘Division and Conflict’ anthology. The map shows that students read extracts from texts such as Birdsong, The Handmaid’s Tale and Twelve Years a Slave. These extracts were chosen to build single-word analysis and selection of evidence. Revisiting them gives you familiar material to rehearse timed analysis. They also model how tension is built through short sentences.
  • Gothic fiction. The map records Gothic fiction in Year 8 and Year 10, featuring Dickens, Stevenson and the Brontës. Gothic writing uses pathetic fallacy, isolated settings and withheld information. These are exactly the techniques examiners expect you to discuss in language and structure answers. Recognising Gothic conventions helps you explain why a setting feels threatening.
  • Film and camera work. A film director moves from establishing shot to close-up to guide the audience’s attention. Writers do the same with structure. Thinking in ‘camera’ terms (wide, narrow, cut-away, flashback) gives you clear vocabulary for the structure question. It also prevents you from drifting into word-level analysis. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Weather reports versus fiction. Compare a factual forecast (“heavy rain is expected”) with a fictional storm (“the sky bled”). The forecast informs; the fiction creates emotion through imagery. This contrast is a quick way to show you understand what makes literary language effective. Use it to check that your analysis always reaches the effect on the reader. [to be confirmed by teacher]
Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • I strongly agree with the statement, because the writer’s imagery consistently presents the setting as hostile.
  • The writer most strikingly creates tension through the shift from wide description to a single, isolated figure.
  • Although the opening seems calm, this calm is a deliberate contrast that makes the later threat more shocking.
  • I only partly agree, since the character’s fear is clear but the setting itself is presented with some beauty.
  • The writer’s use of short sentences at the climax is highly effective because it mirrors the character’s panic.
  • Overall, the statement is convincing, as both the language and the structure build a growing sense of menace.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Box the line references for every question before you start writing.
  2. In retrieval, write facts only — no inference.
  3. In language, zoom in on single words and explain connotations.
  4. In structure, track beginning, middle and end and explain each shift of focus.
  5. In evaluation, state your view first, test it with counter-evidence, then give a weighted judgement.
  6. Use the reader’s response as the endpoint of every analytical sentence.
