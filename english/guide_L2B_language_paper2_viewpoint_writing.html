Concepts

Content

L2B.1 Persuasive writing

Key definitionRhetoric: language crafted to persuade, e.g. direct address, rhetorical questions, anecdote, tricolon.

Identify form (article, letter, speech), audience and purpose. Build a clear argument with a counter-argument and a strong ending.

ExampleSpeech opening: “Imagine a school where every voice is heard. Now imagine it is ours.”
Common mistakeListing devices without a coherent argument.
Evaluation pointAcknowledging and rebutting an opposing view makes the argument stronger.
Exam linkSection B gives a statement and asks you to write in a specified form.

L2B.2 Form conventions

Key definitionForm: the type of text and its conventions.

Letter: greeting and sign-off. Article: headline and subheadings optional. Speech: address the audience.

ExampleA letter to a headteacher should be formal: “Dear Headteacher… Yours faithfully”.
Common mistakeIgnoring the specified form.
Evaluation pointMatching register to audience is part of the mark.
Exam linkThe form is always named in the task.

L2B.3 Building an argument

Key definitionArgument structure: an ordered sequence of points leading to a clear conclusion.

ParagraphPurpose
OpeningHook and clear position
Point 1Strongest supporting reason with evidence or anecdote
Point 2Second reason, developed
Counter-argumentAcknowledge and rebut the opposing view
ConclusionCall to action or memorable final line

Each paragraph should develop one idea fully. Link paragraphs with discourse markers (“Furthermore”, “Admittedly”, “Ultimately”). The map stresses persuasive and non-fiction writing developed through KS3; this structure builds on that.

ExampleArgument: homework should be reduced. Counter: some say it builds discipline. Rebuttal: discipline grows better through independent reading chosen by students.
Common mistakeA list of disconnected opinions lacks logical progression.
Evaluation pointA rebutted counter-argument signals a mature, balanced writer.
Exam linkOrganisation is assessed alongside content [weighting to confirm].

L2B.4 Register and audience

Key definitionRegister: the level of formality chosen for a specific audience.

A broadsheet article needs formal, measured language; a speech to students can be more direct. Match vocabulary, tone and sentence style to the named audience. Avoid slang unless deliberately quoted for effect.

ExampleTo a council: “I urge the committee to reconsider.” To peers: “Let’s be honest — we deserve better.”
Common mistakeUsing the same register for every task.
Evaluation pointRegister control demonstrates awareness of audience and purpose.
Exam linkThe audience is named in the task.

Diagrams

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. No counter-argument. Why it is wrong: A one-sided piece seems less convincing and less sophisticated. Instead: Acknowledge and rebut one opposing view.
  2. Wrong register for the audience. Why it is wrong: Mismatched tone undermines credibility. Instead: Re-read the task and choose formality accordingly.
  3. Overusing devices. Why it is wrong: Device-stuffing feels artificial and distracts from argument. Instead: Use a few devices purposefully.
  4. Ignoring the form. Why it is wrong: Form conventions are part of meeting the task. Instead: Include features of the named form.
  5. No plan. Why it is wrong: Arguments drift and repeat. Instead: Plan five paragraphs before writing.

Colour-Coded Model Answers

AO1 Identify / interpret / synthesiseAO2 Language & structure / methodsAO3 Compare viewpoints & methodsAO4 Evaluate critically / AO5–6 writing [AO labels to be confirmed by teacher]Connective

Extended writing: Write a speech arguing that young people should have a voice in local decisions.[tariff to confirm]

Imagine a town where every voice is heard. Now imagine it is ours. Some argue that teenagers lack experience; however, we will live with today’s decisions longest. Surely, then, we deserve a seat at the table.

What makes this workHook, counter-argument with rebuttal, and a rhetorical question as a call to action.
Extended writing: Write a letter to your headteacher arguing for a change in school policy.[tariff to confirm]

Dear Headteacher, I am writing to propose a student-led reading hour. Admittedly, timetables are full; yet twenty minutes of reading daily would strengthen every subject. Yours faithfully, …

What makes this workCorrect letter form, formal register and a rebutted objection.
Examples

Real-World & Context Examples

  • Year 9 Protest speeches. The map highlights speeches from contemporary and historical figures, including from the Black community. These model powerful rhetoric. Study how they use repetition and direct address. Adapt the techniques, not the content, for exam tasks.
  • Spoken Language Endorsement. Students research and present a speech on a current issue in Year 10. That preparation transfers directly to written speeches in Section B. The same structure of hook, argument and conclusion applies. Rehearsing aloud also improves sentence rhythm. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Newspaper editorials. Editorials present a reasoned view in formal register. They usually include a counter-argument. They are the best model for ‘article’ tasks. Note how they open with a topical hook. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Letters to MPs or councils. Campaign letters use formal address and clear requests. They show how to balance politeness with firmness. These conventions are what examiners look for in letter tasks. Practise writing one on a local issue. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Debate clubs. The map lists Debate Mate as an English enrichment activity. Debating trains you to anticipate counter-arguments. This is exactly the skill that lifts a Section B response. Use debate practice to rehearse rebuttals.
Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • Admittedly, there are costs to this proposal, yet the long-term benefits clearly outweigh them.
  • It is undeniable that change is difficult; however, staying the same would be far more damaging.
  • Critics may claim that young people are uninformed, but this underestimates their awareness of the issues.
  • Surely, then, the only reasonable course of action is to act now.
  • Ultimately, the choice is clear: we must choose fairness over convenience.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Identify form, audience and purpose first.
  2. Plan five paragraphs.
  3. Include and rebut a counter-argument.
  4. Match register to audience.
  5. Use devices sparingly and purposefully.
  6. Proofread.
