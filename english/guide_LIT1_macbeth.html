Concepts

Content

LIT1.1 The play form

Key definitionPlay form: drama written for performance — soliloquies, asides, stage directions and dramatic irony.

Soliloquies reveal private thoughts; the audience often knows more than characters (dramatic irony).

ExampleMacbeth’s “Is this a dagger” soliloquy exposes his disordered mind.
Common mistakeCalling it a ‘book’ or ‘novel’.
Evaluation pointRefer to the audience and performance.
Exam linkExtract-based essays ask about the extract and the whole play.

LIT1.2 The Tragic Hero

Key definitionTragic hero: a noble figure brought down by a fatal flaw (hamartia).

Macbeth’s ambition is his hamartia; his fall moves from brave warrior to tyrant.

Example“Brave Macbeth” (Act 1) contrasts with “this dead butcher” (Act 5).
Common mistakeTreating Macbeth as simply evil from the start.
Evaluation pointIs Macbeth driven by ambition, the witches, or Lady Macbeth? Weigh all three.
Exam linkQuestions frequently ask how Shakespeare presents ambition or Macbeth as a character.

LIT1.3 Key characters

Key definitionCharacterisation: the methods a writer uses to present and develop a character.

CharacterRoleKey quotationDevelopment
MacbethTragic hero“Vaulting ambition, which o’erleaps itself”From loyal warrior to paranoid tyrant
Lady MacbethInstigator“unsex me here”From dominant to guilt-ridden and sleepwalking
The WitchesSupernatural catalyst“Fair is foul, and foul is fair”Equivocating prophecies that tempt
BanquoFoil to Macbeth“instruments of darkness tell us truths”Resists temptation; murdered; returns as ghost
MacduffAvenger“from his mother’s womb untimely ripp’d”Grief-stricken father who kills Macbeth
DuncanIdeal king“There’s no art to find the mind’s construction in the face”Trusting; murdered in Act 2

Banquo is the most important foil: he hears the same prophecy but does not act on it, showing that Macbeth’s fall is a choice. Lady Macbeth’s reversal — she calls on spirits to remove her femininity, yet later cannot wash imagined blood from her hands — shows the psychological cost of guilt.

ExampleLady Macbeth’s “Out, damned spot!” in Act 5 reverses her earlier “A little water clears us of this deed”.
Common mistakeTreating Lady Macbeth as simply evil ignores her collapse.
Evaluation pointArguably she is as much a victim of ambition as Macbeth.
Exam linkCharacter questions often ask about Lady Macbeth or Macbeth’s change.

LIT1.4 Themes: ambition, guilt, the supernatural, kingship

Key definitionTheme: a central idea explored across a text.

Ambition drives the tragedy. Macbeth admits he has only “vaulting ambition” to spur him; Shakespeare presents unchecked ambition as self-destructive.

Guilt appears through hallucinations (the dagger, Banquo’s ghost), sleeplessness (“Macbeth does murder sleep”) and Lady Macbeth’s sleepwalking. Blood imagery tracks guilt throughout.

The supernatural: the witches speak in paradox and equivocation. They never tell Macbeth to kill, which raises the question of free will versus fate.

Kingship: Duncan and later Malcolm represent legitimate kingship; Macbeth’s tyranny brings disorder to nature itself (horses eating each other, darkness by day).

ExampleAfter Duncan’s murder, Ross reports that “by th’ clock ’tis day, / And yet dark night strangles the travelling lamp” — nature reflects disorder.
Common mistakeDiscussing themes without tracing them across the play.
Evaluation pointThe witches tempt but do not compel — so responsibility remains Macbeth’s.
Exam linkTheme questions are common in the extract-based essay.

LIT1.5 Context: Jacobean England

Key definitionDivine right of kings: belief that monarchs were appointed by God.

The play was written around 1606, early in James I’s reign. James had written Daemonologie on witchcraft and claimed descent from Banquo. The Gunpowder Plot of 1605 made regicide a live fear. Shakespeare presents Banquo sympathetically and regicide as a sin against God and nature.

Gender expectations of the time cast women as subordinate; Lady Macbeth’s rejection of femininity would have seemed deeply unnatural to the audience.

ExampleMacbeth’s killing of Duncan disrupts the Great Chain of Being, so nature itself is thrown into disorder.
Common mistakeWriting a history paragraph unrelated to the question.
Evaluation pointContext should explain why Shakespeare makes a choice.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Ignoring the extract. Why it is wrong: The question starts with the extract; failing to analyse it limits the response. Instead: Spend roughly the first third analysing the extract closely.
  2. Retelling the plot. Why it is wrong: Narrative earns little credit compared with analysis. Instead: Make arguments about Shakespeare’s methods and purposes.
  3. Bolted-on context. Why it is wrong: Isolated history paragraphs do not answer the question. Instead: Link context to a specific choice in a sentence.
  4. Not naming Shakespeare as a deliberate writer. Why it is wrong: The question is about how the writer presents ideas. Instead: Use “Shakespeare presents…”, “Shakespeare uses…”.
  5. Treating Macbeth as simply evil from the start. Why it is wrong: This ignores his tragic fall and early nobility. Instead: Trace his change across the play.

Analytical — point / evidence / analysis / context: Starting with this extract, explore how Shakespeare presents ambition.[tariff to confirm]

POINT: Shakespeare presents ambition as dangerously self-destructive. EVIDENCE: Macbeth has only “vaulting ambition, which o’erleaps itself”. ANALYSIS: the image of a rider leaping too far and falling suggests that ambition carries the seeds of its own failure. CONTEXT: for a Jacobean audience, ambition that threatened a king was sinful, therefore Macbeth’s fall is presented as inevitable.

Context points in this answer: [to be confirmed by teacher]

What makes this workPoint-evidence-analysis-context chain with a clear argument.
Evaluative — FOR / AGAINST / weighted judgement: To what extent is Macbeth responsible for his own downfall?[tariff to confirm]

FOR: Macbeth chooses to murder Duncan despite listing reasons not to; this shows his downfall is self-made. AGAINST: however, the witches and Lady Macbeth manipulate him; her taunt “When you durst do it, then you were a man” pressures his masculinity. WEIGHTED JUDGEMENT: yet Banquo hears the same prophecy and does not act, so ultimately Macbeth bears the greatest responsibility.

What makes this workFor, against and a weighted judgement using Banquo as decisive evidence.
Examples

Real-World & Context Examples

  • The Gunpowder Plot (1605). Catholic conspirators attempted to blow up Parliament and James I. The plot made regicide a vivid fear for the original audience. Shakespeare’s presentation of Duncan’s murder as an unnatural crime reflects this fear. Using this context explains why the play punishes Macbeth so completely. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • James I and witchcraft. James wrote Daemonologie (1597) and took part in witch trials. The witches’ presence would have been genuinely frightening to the audience. Shakespeare presents them as agents of disorder. This helps explain why their prophecies are so destructive.
  • Divine right and the Great Chain of Being. Monarchs were believed to be chosen by God, at the top of an ordered hierarchy. Killing a king therefore disrupted the whole natural order. This explains the unnatural events after Duncan’s death. It also explains why Malcolm’s restoration ends the play with order regained. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Banquo as James’s ancestor. James I claimed descent from Banquo. Shakespeare presents Banquo as honourable, resisting the witches’ temptation. This flatters the king and creates a moral contrast with Macbeth. Use this when discussing Banquo as a foil. [to be confirmed by teacher]
  • Year 9 ‘Romeo and Juliet’ and Year 8 ‘The Tempest’. The map shows these plays prepare students for Macbeth. They introduce Elizabethan and Jacobean context, crime and punishment, and power. Revisiting them strengthens your understanding of Shakespeare’s methods. Connections show breadth of knowledge.
Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • Shakespeare ultimately suggests that unchecked ambition destroys both the individual and the natural order.
  • Arguably, Macbeth is less a villain than a tragic figure whose noble qualities are corrupted.
  • Although the witches tempt Macbeth, Banquo’s restraint shows that the final choice is Macbeth’s own.
  • A Jacobean audience would have seen Macbeth’s death as the necessary restoration of divine order.
  • Lady Macbeth’s collapse suggests that guilt cannot be suppressed, however strong the will.
  • Therefore, the play functions as a warning about the moral cost of ambition.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Analyse the extract first, then range across the whole play.
  2. Embed short quotations.
  3. Integrate context into argument sentences.
  4. Track character change across all five acts.
  5. Use Banquo as a foil.
