Concepts

3.3.1 The Problem of Evil

Key definitionProblem of evil: if God is omnipotent and omnibenevolent, why does evil exist?

Moral evil is caused by humans; natural evil comes from nature. The problem challenges belief in God's attributes. The problem is sometimes called the inconsistent triad because the three claims seem not to fit together.

Common mistakeNot distinguishing moral and natural evil.
Evaluation pointIs the problem of evil the strongest argument against God? Many philosophers think so.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the problem of evil; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.3.2 Divergent solutions to the problem of evil

Key definitionTheodicy: a justification of God in the face of evil.

Christian responses include free will, soul-making (Irenaeus), and the Fall (Augustine), as well as prayer and service as practical responses. Many Christians also respond practically, through prayer and helping those who suffer.

Common mistakeOnly giving one theodicy.
Evaluation pointDoes free will justify natural evil? It struggles to.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about divergent solutions to the problem of evil; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.3.3 The Design argument

Key definitionDesign (teleological) argument: the world's order shows a designer.

Paley's watch analogy is the classic version; critics point to evolution and to flaws in nature. Christians who accept evolution may argue that God designed the process itself.

Common mistakeConfusing design and cosmological arguments.
Evaluation pointDoes evolution defeat Paley? It removes the need for a designer of species but not of the laws of nature.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the design argument; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.3.4 The Cosmological argument

Key definitionCosmological argument: the universe needs a first cause.

Aquinas argued there must be an uncaused first cause, God; critics ask what caused God or suggest the universe may have no beginning. Supporters argue that an infinite chain of causes explains nothing, so a first cause is needed.

Common mistakeSaying the argument proves the Christian God specifically.
Evaluation pointIs a first cause necessarily God? Critics say not.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the cosmological argument; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.3.5 Religious Upbringing

Key definitionReligious upbringing: being raised in a faith.

Christian upbringing includes infant baptism, Sunday school and confirmation; critics argue it may limit free choice, while supporters see it as giving foundations. Infant baptism involves parents and godparents making promises to raise the child in the faith.

Common mistakeTreating upbringing only negatively.
Evaluation pointDoes religious upbringing remove choice? Confirmation suggests a later free decision.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about religious upbringing; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Moral vs natural evil. Define both with examples.
  2. Theodicy names. Augustine (Fall) and Irenaeus (soul-making).
  3. Arguments confused. Design = order/purpose; cosmological = cause.
  4. 'Proves God'. Arguments support, not prove.
  5. Upbringing one-sided. Show for and against.

Answers

Colour-Coded Model Answers

Key: AO1 knowledgeAO2 analysis / evaluationconnective

Outline three Christian beliefs about the problem of evil. (3)3 marks
  1. Problem of evil: if God is omnipotent and omnibenevolent, why does evil exist?
  2. Theodicy: a justification of God in the face of evil.
  3. Design (teleological) argument: the world's order shows a designer.
What makes this workThree separate, accurate points; no development is needed, which saves time for the 12-mark question.
Explain two reasons why the problem of evil challenges belief in God. (4)4 marks

If God is omnipotent, God could stop evil; this means that the existence of evil suggests God is not all-powerful.

If God is omnibenevolent, God would want to stop evil, which shows that evil may suggest God is not all-loving.

What makes this workTwo distinct points, each developed with a connective that explains why it matters.
Explain two Christian responses to the problem of evil. (5)5 marks

Some Christians argue evil comes from human free will; this means that God allows evil so humans can choose good.

Irenaeus taught that suffering helps souls grow towards God, which shows that evil can have a purpose.

Source of wisdom and authority: Irenaeus' soul-making theodicy: confirm scholar and wording with teacher

What makes this workTwo developed points plus a named source of wisdom and authority that directly supports one of them. The exact source wording is marked for teacher confirmation.
Evaluate: 'The design argument proves God exists.' (12)12 marks

FOR (1): The order of the universe suggests a designer; this is because complex things like Paley's watch are designed.

FOR (2): Fine-tuning of physical constants suggests the universe is suited to life; as a result many find design more probable than chance.

AGAINST (1): However, evolution explains apparent design naturally, and therefore a designer is not required.

AGAINST (2): Natural evil and poor design in nature, such as disease, could lead to the view that a perfect designer is unlikely.

WEIGHTED JUDGEMENT: Overall, the argument is suggestive but not proof.

What makes this workTwo developed points on each side, each a chain of reasoning with highlighted connectives; the judgement weighs the sides and explains why one is stronger rather than simply repeating them. Adding a source of wisdom and authority on each side would strengthen it further.

Examples

Real-World Examples

Example: 2004 Indian Ocean tsunamiKilled over 200,000 people. Used to illustrate natural evil and challenge an omnibenevolent God. In an exam, use it as specific evidence in an evaluative answer on Philosophy of Religion, then explain whether it supports or challenges the statement.
Example: Paley's watchPaley (1802) compared the world to a watch found on a heath. The core example for design. In an exam, use it as specific evidence in an evaluative answer on Philosophy of Religion, then explain whether it supports or challenges the statement.
Example: Big BangChristians such as Georges Lemaître, a Catholic priest, developed the Big Bang theory. Shows science and the cosmological argument can sit together. In an exam, use it as specific evidence in an evaluative answer on Philosophy of Religion, then explain whether it supports or challenges the statement.
Example: ConfirmationMany Anglicans and Catholics are confirmed as teenagers, choosing for themselves. Use against the claim that upbringing removes choice. Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • The free will defence explains moral evil well but struggles to explain natural evil such as earthquakes.
  • Paley's design argument is weakened by evolution, although fine-tuning of the universe keeps a version of it alive.
  • The cosmological argument is strongest when it asks why anything exists at all, rather than what caused the Big Bang.
  • Religious upbringing gives children a foundation, yet critics argue it can limit their freedom to choose.
  • The problem of evil challenges belief in God, but it does not disprove God if a theodicy is accepted.
  • Overall, the arguments for God may support faith but are unlikely to convince a committed atheist.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Inconsistent triad: omnipotent, omnibenevolent, evil exists.
  2. Augustine and Irenaeus.
  3. Paley and evolution.
  4. Aquinas first cause.
  5. Upbringing: baptism, Sunday school, confirmation.
  6. Always balance.
  7. Spend roughly a minute per mark, so leave most time for the 12-mark (d) question.
  8. In (c) answers, name the source precisely and explain how it supports the point.
  9. In (d) answers, give arguments on both sides, include a non-religious or divergent view where relevant, and finish with a justified conclusion.
