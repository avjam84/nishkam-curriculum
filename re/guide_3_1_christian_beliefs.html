Concepts

3.1.1 The Trinity

Key definitionTrinity: one God in three persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

The Nicene Creed expresses the Trinity; each person is fully God, yet there is one God. The doctrine was formally defined in the early councils of the Church.

Common mistakeSaying Christians believe in three gods.
Evaluation pointIs the Trinity coherent? Christians accept it as a mystery revealed by God.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the trinity; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.1.2 Creation of the world

Key definitionCreation: God made the universe out of nothing (ex nihilo).

Genesis 1–2 describes creation; some Christians read it literally, others as symbolic truth compatible with science. John 1 links the Word (Jesus) to creation. Creation shows God's power and goodness, and humans are made in God's image with responsibility for the world.

Common mistakeTreating all Christians as literalists about Genesis.
Evaluation pointIs Genesis compatible with science? Many Christians say it teaches why, not how.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about creation of the world; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.1.3 The Incarnation of Jesus

Key definitionIncarnation: God becoming human in Jesus.

Jesus is believed to be fully God and fully human, shown in the virgin birth accounts in Matthew and Luke. Christians believe the incarnation shows God's love and makes it possible for humans to know God personally.

Common mistakeSaying Jesus was only a good teacher.
Evaluation pointIs the incarnation necessary for salvation? Christians argue only God-as-human can reconcile humanity.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the incarnation of jesus; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.1.4 The Salvation of Jesus

Key definitionSalvation / atonement: being saved from sin and restored to God.

Christians believe Jesus' death and resurrection overcome sin; views differ on how atonement works. Many Christians link salvation to grace, an undeserved gift of God's love.

Common mistakeMixing salvation with general good deeds only.
Evaluation pointIs salvation by faith, works, or both? Protestants and Catholics differ.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the salvation of jesus; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.1.5 Christian Eschatology

Key definitionEschatology: teaching about the end times and the afterlife.

Beliefs include resurrection, judgement, heaven, hell and (for Catholics) purgatory. Judgement is often linked to how people have treated others, as in Jesus' parable of the sheep and goats.

Common mistakePresenting purgatory as all Christians' belief.
Evaluation pointIs hell compatible with a loving God? Some Christians reinterpret hell as separation from God.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about christian eschatology; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.1.6 Revelation

Key definitionRevelation: God making himself known.

General revelation comes through nature and conscience; special revelation through scripture, Jesus and direct experience. Christians believe the Bible is the main source of special revelation.

Common mistakeNot distinguishing general and special revelation.
Evaluation pointIs revelation reliable? Hard to verify, but believers point to its effects.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about revelation; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.1.7 Visions

Key definitionVision: a religious experience of seeing something sacred.

Examples include visions in the Bible such as Saul on the Damascus road; critics question whether visions are genuine. Visions are often tested against scripture and Church teaching before being accepted.

Common mistakeAccepting all visions as genuine.
Evaluation pointCould visions be hallucinations? Sceptics say yes; believers point to life change.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about visions; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.1.8 Miracles

Key definitionMiracle: an event that seems to break the laws of nature, attributed to God.

Gospel miracles show Jesus' authority; some claim modern healings (e.g. at Lourdes) while sceptics such as Hume question the evidence. Christians debate whether miracles still happen today or were mainly signs during Jesus' ministry.

Common mistakeDefining miracle vaguely as 'something good'.
Evaluation pointIs Hume right that testimony is never enough? Believers say he rules miracles out in advance.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about miracles; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.1.9 Religious experiences

Key definitionReligious experience: a direct encounter with God or the sacred.

Types include conversion, visions and numinous experiences; they may strengthen faith but are hard to verify. Conversion experiences can change a person's whole direction of life.

Common mistakeNot naming types of experience.
Evaluation pointDo religious experiences prove God? They may convince the individual but not others.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about religious experiences; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

3.1.10 Christian teachings about prayers

Key definitionPrayer: communication with God.

The Lord's Prayer is Jesus' model; prayer may be adoration, confession, thanksgiving or supplication. Unanswered prayer raises questions about God. Prayer may be private or public, spoken or silent.

Common mistakeIgnoring unanswered prayer.
Evaluation pointDoes unanswered prayer disprove God? Christians say God answers in his own way.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about christian teachings about prayers; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Christian beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. 'Three gods'. The Trinity is one God in three persons.
  2. All Christians literalists. Show divergent views on Genesis.
  3. Weak source use. Use the Nicene Creed, Genesis 1, John 1.
  4. Hume misrepresented. He argued against believing reports of miracles, not that they are logically impossible.
  5. Salvation views not distinguished. Mention grace, faith and works.

Answers

Colour-Coded Model Answers

Key: AO1 knowledgeAO2 analysis / evaluationconnective

Outline three Christian beliefs about the trinity. (3)3 marks
  1. Trinity: one God in three persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  2. Creation: God made the universe out of nothing (ex nihilo).
  3. Incarnation: God becoming human in Jesus.
What makes this workThree separate, accurate points; no development is needed, which saves time for the 12-mark question.
Explain two Christian beliefs about the incarnation. (4)4 marks

Christians believe Jesus was fully God and fully human; this means that he could understand human suffering and show what God is like.

The incarnation began with the virgin birth, which shows that Jesus' life was the work of God.

What makes this workTwo distinct points, each developed with a connective that explains why it matters.
Explain two Christian beliefs about the Trinity. (5)5 marks

Christians believe God is one in three persons; this means that Father, Son and Holy Spirit are each fully God, as stated in the Nicene Creed.

The Holy Spirit is God at work in the world, which shows that God is still present and active for believers.

Source of wisdom and authority: Nicene Creed: exact wording to be supplied by teacher

What makes this workTwo developed points plus a named source of wisdom and authority that directly supports one of them. The exact source wording is marked for teacher confirmation.
Evaluate: 'Miracles prove that God exists.' (12)12 marks

FOR (1): Healings at Lourdes have been investigated and not explained medically; this shows that some events are hard to explain without God.

FOR (2): Miracles in the Gospels are presented as signs of Jesus' authority; this means that, for believers, they point to God's power.

AGAINST (1): However, Hume argued that reports of miracles are less likely than mistakes or deception, and therefore they cannot prove God.

AGAINST (2): Many people who pray for healing are not healed, which could lead to the view that apparent miracles are coincidence.

WEIGHTED JUDGEMENT: Overall, miracles may support faith but cannot prove God to a sceptic.

What makes this workTwo developed points on each side, each a chain of reasoning with highlighted connectives; the judgement weighs the sides and explains why one is stronger rather than simply repeating them. Adding a source of wisdom and authority on each side would strengthen it further.

Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • The Trinity is a mystery, but this does not make it incoherent, because the three persons share one divine nature.
  • Revelation is convincing to believers, though critics argue that experiences of God cannot be checked by others.
  • Hume's challenge to miracle reports is strong, yet it seems to rule out miracles before looking at the evidence.
  • Religious experience may strengthen faith rather than prove God, since its value is mainly for the person who has it.
  • Belief in the incarnation underpins Christian teaching on salvation, because only God as human could reconcile humanity to God.
  • Overall, Christian beliefs form a connected system, so weakening one belief affects the others.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Trinity: one God, three persons.
  2. Creation ex nihilo; Genesis 1–2, John 1.
  3. Incarnation: fully God, fully human.
  4. Salvation through Jesus' death and resurrection.
  5. General vs special revelation.
  6. Hume against miracle reports.
  7. Spend roughly a minute per mark, so leave most time for the 12-mark (d) question.
  8. In (c) answers, name the source precisely and explain how it supports the point.
  9. In (d) answers, give arguments on both sides, include a non-religious or divergent view where relevant, and finish with a justified conclusion.
