Concepts

1.3.1 The importance and purpose of marriage

Key definitionAnand Karaj: the Sikh marriage ceremony, 'blissful union'.

Marriage unites two souls and two families and is seen as a spiritual partnership. The couple walk around the Guru Granth Sahib four times during the Lavan. In the ceremony each Lav is read and sung before the couple walk around the scripture, showing the couple's journey towards God together.

Common mistakeSaying Anand Karaj is just a contract; Sikhs see it as a spiritual union.
Evaluation pointIs marriage still important when cohabitation is common? Sikhs argue it gives commitment and family stability.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the importance and purpose of marriage; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.3.2 The importance of sexual relationships

Key definitionSex within marriage as part of a committed union.

Sikh teaching sees sex as belonging within marriage; lust (kaam) is one of the five thieves when it is uncontrolled. Self-control over sexual desire is seen as part of overcoming the five thieves.

Common mistakeImplying all sexual activity is condemned; it is lust (kaam) out of control that is a thief.
Evaluation pointIs sex before marriage always wrong? Sikh teaching says yes; non-religious views often disagree.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the importance of sexual relationships; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.

1.3.3 The importance of Family life

Key definitionGrihasth: the householder stage of life.

The Gurus lived as householders, showing that spiritual life is lived in family and work, not by withdrawing from the world. Children learn Sikh values first in the home, which makes the family the first place of religious education.

Common mistakeOverlooking the householder ideal in favour of withdrawal.
Evaluation pointIs family life a help or a distraction to spiritual life? The Gurus' example says help.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the importance of family life; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.

1.3.4 Support for the family in the community

Key definitionCommunity support provided by the gurdwara and sangat.

The gurdwara supports families through classes, the langar, ceremonies and pastoral help. Gurdwaras may offer advice to couples and support the elderly, the bereaved and new parents.

Common mistakeNot giving specific examples of community support.
Evaluation pointShould family support be a religious or state responsibility? Both play a part.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about support for the family in the community; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.3.5 Contraception

Key definitionContraception: methods used to prevent pregnancy.

There is no single ruling; many Sikhs accept contraception within marriage as a decision for the couple, though some oppose methods that may end a fertilised life. Some Sikhs argue that family size is not a religious matter, while others are cautious about methods they see as harming life.

Common mistakeStating Sikhism bans contraception outright; there is no single ruling.
Evaluation pointIs contraception a personal or religious decision? Many Sikhs leave it to the couple.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about contraception; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.3.6 Teachings about divorce

Key definitionDivorce: legal ending of a marriage.

Marriage is intended to be lifelong, so divorce is discouraged; families and the sangat should try to reconcile the couple, but divorce is permitted under UK law and remarriage may happen. Divorce often brings shame in some communities, though Sikh teaching emphasises compassion.

Common mistakeSaying Sikhs never divorce; it is discouraged, not impossible.
Evaluation pointIs reconciliation always best? Not where there is abuse; many Sikhs accept divorce then.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about teachings about divorce; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.3.7 Equality of men and women in the family

Key definitionEquality of genders because the same divine light is in all.

Guru Nanak taught that women should not be called inferior, as they give birth to kings. Men and women share roles in the family. The Gurus criticised practices such as sati and female infanticide, showing concern for women's status.

Common mistakeAssuming equality in teaching means equality in all practice.
Evaluation pointHas Sikh teaching achieved equality in families? Teaching is clear; practice varies.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about equality of men and women in the family; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.3.8 Divergent teachings about gender prejudice and discrimination

Key definitionGender prejudice / discrimination: unfair judgement / treatment because of sex.

Sikh teaching rejects gender discrimination, but students should note the divergence between teaching and some cultural practice, and compare with other views. Students should show that Sikh scripture opposes discrimination, while acknowledging that inequality persists in some communities.

Common mistakeNot presenting divergent views.
Evaluation pointAre cultural practices or scripture a better guide? Scripture is the authority for most Sikhs.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about divergent teachings about gender prejudice and discrimination; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Calling the Lavan 'vows'. They are four hymns read as the couple circle the Guru Granth Sahib.
  2. Saying Sikhism bans divorce. It is discouraged, not forbidden.
  3. One Sikh view on contraception. Show variety.
  4. Ignoring UK law. Use the Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013 and divorce law for context.
  5. Gender answers without a source. Use Guru Nanak's teaching on women.

Example question stems (written for this site, not Edexcel questions)

  • Outline three Sikh beliefs about the importance and purpose of marriage. (3)
  • Explain two reasons why the importance of sexual relationships is important to Sikhs. (4)
  • Explain two Sikh teachings about the importance of family life. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority. (5)
  • 'Divergent teachings about gender prejudice and discrimination' – a 12-mark statement on this sub-topic, e.g. 'Divorce is always wrong.' Evaluate this statement. (12)
Answers

Colour-Coded Model Answers

Key: AO1 knowledgeAO2 analysis / evaluationconnective

Outline three Sikh beliefs about the importance and purpose of marriage. (3)3 marks
  1. Anand Karaj: the Sikh marriage ceremony, 'blissful union'.
  2. Sex within marriage as part of a committed union.
  3. Grihasth: the householder stage of life.
What makes this workThree separate, accurate points; no development is needed, which saves time for the 12-mark question.
Explain two reasons why marriage is important to Sikhs. (4)4 marks

Marriage is a spiritual union of two souls; this means that the couple support each other on the path to God.

Marriage joins two families as well as two people, which shows that it strengthens the wider community.

What makes this workTwo distinct points, each developed with a connective that explains why it matters.
Explain two Sikh teachings about the equality of men and women. (5)5 marks

Guru Nanak taught that women should not be called inferior, because 'from her, kings are born'; this means that women have equal dignity.

Men and women may both lead worship and read from the Guru Granth Sahib, which shows that equality is meant to apply in religious life.

Source of wisdom and authority: Guru Nanak's teaching on women: exact verse reference to be supplied by teacher

What makes this workTwo developed points plus a named source of wisdom and authority that directly supports one of them. The exact source wording is marked for teacher confirmation.
Evaluate: 'Divorce is always wrong.' (12)12 marks

FOR (1): Marriage is a spiritual union before the Guru Granth Sahib; this means that ending it breaks a sacred commitment.

FOR (2): Sikh marriages are conducted before the Guru Granth Sahib, which shows that the commitment is made to God as well as to the partner.

AGAINST (1): However, where a marriage involves abuse, staying could cause harm, and therefore many Sikhs and non-religious people accept divorce.

AGAINST (2): UK law allows divorce, and many Sikhs accept it for serious reasons such as abuse; this means that an absolute view is too harsh.

WEIGHTED JUDGEMENT: Overall, divorce is a last resort rather than always wrong; the statement is too absolute.

What makes this workTwo developed points on each side, each a chain of reasoning with highlighted connectives; the judgement weighs the sides and explains why one is stronger rather than simply repeating them. Adding a source of wisdom and authority on each side would strengthen it further.

Examples

Real-World Examples

Example: Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013Same-sex marriage became legal in England and Wales in 2014. Many Sikh authorities do not perform same-sex Anand Karaj, showing a divergence between religious and secular practice. In an exam, use it as specific evidence in an evaluative answer on Marriage and the Family, then explain whether it supports or challenges the statement.
Example: No-fault divorceThe Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 introduced no-fault divorce in England and Wales from 2022. This contrasts with religious emphasis on reconciliation. In an exam, use it as specific evidence in an evaluative answer on Marriage and the Family, then explain whether it supports or challenges the statement.
Example: Mai BhagoAn 18th-century Sikh woman who led soldiers in battle. She is used as an example of women's equal status in Sikh history. In an exam, use it as specific evidence in an evaluative answer on Marriage and the Family, then explain whether it supports or challenges the statement.
Example: Gurdwara family supportMany gurdwaras run Punjabi and Gurmat classes for children and support for elderly members, showing community support for families. In an exam, use it as specific evidence in an evaluative answer on Marriage and the Family, then explain whether it supports or challenges the statement.
Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • Sikh teaching on gender equality is clear, but cultural practice in some families shows that it has not always been followed.
  • Marriage gives stability to children and families, yet non-religious critics argue that commitment does not need a ceremony.
  • Divorce is discouraged in Sikhism, but compassion for people in harmful marriages makes an absolute ban unconvincing.
  • Contraception is best seen as a decision for the couple, because there is no single ruling in Sikh teaching.
  • The householder ideal explains why family life is central to Sikhism rather than a distraction from it.
  • Overall, the sangat's support for families shows that marriage is a community concern as well as a private one.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Anand Karaj: four Lavan around the Guru Granth Sahib.
  2. Grihasth: householder ideal.
  3. Divorce discouraged, possible.
  4. No single ruling on contraception.
  5. Guru Nanak on women: 'from her kings are born'.
  6. Always include a non-religious or divergent view in (d).
  7. Spend roughly a minute per mark, so leave most time for the 12-mark (d) question.
  8. In (c) answers, name the source precisely and explain how it supports the point.
  9. In (d) answers, give arguments on both sides, include a non-religious or divergent view where relevant, and finish with a justified conclusion.
