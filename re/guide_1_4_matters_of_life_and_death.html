Concepts

1.4.1 The origins of the world

Key definitionHukam: God's will, through which the universe was created.

Sikh scripture describes God creating the universe by divine will, and many Sikhs see no conflict with scientific accounts. Sikh scripture speaks of countless worlds and universes, which some Sikhs see as consistent with modern cosmology.

Common mistakeSaying Sikhs reject science; many accept it alongside hukam.
Evaluation pointDoes science leave room for God? Many Sikhs say science explains how, faith why.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the origins of the world; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.4.2 The sanctity of life

Key definitionSanctity of life: life is sacred because it comes from God.

Because God's light is in all life, Sikhs believe life should be protected; this shapes views on abortion and euthanasia. The sanctity of life also leads many Sikhs to oppose suicide and to value medical care.

Common mistakeUsing sanctity of life without explaining why life is sacred.
Evaluation pointIs quality of life more important than sanctity? Sikhs prioritise sanctity but show compassion.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the sanctity of life; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.4.3 Scientific and non-religious explanations about the world

Key definitionBig Bang theory and evolution.

Scientific explanations account for origins without reference to God; non-religious people may use them to reject a creator, while some religious people combine them with belief. Evolution explains how life developed over time through natural selection.

Common mistakePresenting science as automatically anti-religious.
Evaluation pointDoes the Big Bang disprove creation? Not necessarily: it may describe God's method.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about scientific and non-religious explanations about the world; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.4.4 The issue of abortion

Key definitionAbortion: the deliberate ending of a pregnancy.

Many Sikhs oppose abortion as it ends a life given by God, but some accept it in cases such as risk to the mother's life. UK law permits abortion under conditions in the Abortion Act 1967. Abortion is often seen as harmful karma, because it ends a life that God has given.

Common mistakeSaying abortion is always banned in Sikhism; most oppose it, some accept exceptions.
Evaluation pointShould the mother's life take priority? Many Sikhs accept this exception.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the issue of abortion; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.4.5 The existence of death and the afterlife

Key definitionRebirth: the soul is reborn after death until mukti.

Death is the end of one life in the cycle of samsara; the funeral is a time for kirtan and the body is usually cremated. Death is seen as a natural part of God's hukam, so mourning is restrained and hymns are sung.

Common mistakeMixing Christian heaven/hell with Sikh rebirth.
Evaluation pointIs rebirth more just than one life? Sikhs see it as giving repeated chances.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the existence of death and the afterlife; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.4.6 Non-religious arguments against life after death

Key definitionArguments that consciousness depends on the brain.

Humanists and atheists argue there is no evidence the mind survives death, and that claims of near-death experiences can be explained by brain activity. Non-religious people may find meaning in this life only, without belief in reward or punishment.

Common mistakeGiving humanist views without reasons.
Evaluation pointIs lack of evidence enough to reject an afterlife? Absence of evidence is not proof of absence.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about non-religious arguments against life after death; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.4.7 The issue of euthanasia

Key definitionEuthanasia: ending a life to relieve suffering.

Many Sikhs oppose euthanasia because suffering may be karma and life is God's gift; care and sewa for the dying are emphasised. Euthanasia is illegal in the UK, though assisted dying has been debated in Parliament. Many Sikhs see medical help to manage pain as acceptable while rejecting deliberately ending life.

Common mistakeCalling euthanasia legal in the UK; it is not.
Evaluation pointIs suffering ever meaningful? Some Sikhs say karma; others stress relief of pain through care.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the issue of euthanasia; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.4.8 Issues in the natural world

Key definitionStewardship: responsibility to care for creation.

Because God is in creation, Sikhs believe in protecting the environment and animals; many Sikhs are vegetarian, and the langar serves vegetarian food. Sikh teaching sees the natural world as a reflection of God, so pollution and waste go against belief.

Common mistakeGeneric environmental answers with no Sikh belief.
Evaluation pointIs vegetarianism required? Langar food is vegetarian; personal practice varies.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about issues in the natural world; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Euthanasia legal in UK. It is illegal; assisted dying has been debated.
  2. Confusing abortion law. Abortion Act 1967: up to 24 weeks with two doctors' agreement, with exceptions.
  3. Christian afterlife in Sikh answers. Use samsara and mukti.
  4. Science vs religion only. Show they can be compatible.
  5. No non-religious view. Required in many (d) questions.

Example question stems (written for this site, not Edexcel questions)

  • Outline three Sikh beliefs about the origins of the world. (3)
  • Explain two reasons why the sanctity of life is important to Sikhs. (4)
  • Explain two Sikh teachings about scientific and non-religious explanations about the world. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority. (5)
  • 'Issues in the natural world' – a 12-mark statement on this sub-topic, e.g. 'Euthanasia should be legal.' Evaluate this statement. (12)
Answers

Colour-Coded Model Answers

Key: AO1 knowledgeAO2 analysis / evaluationconnective

Outline three Sikh beliefs about the origins of the world. (3)3 marks
  1. Hukam: God's will, through which the universe was created.
  2. Sanctity of life: life is sacred because it comes from God.
  3. Big Bang theory and evolution.
What makes this workThree separate, accurate points; no development is needed, which saves time for the 12-mark question.
Explain two Sikh beliefs about euthanasia. (4)4 marks

Many Sikhs oppose euthanasia because life is a gift from God; this means that only God should decide when it ends.

Some Sikhs believe suffering may be the result of karma, which could lead to the view that it should be endured with support rather than ended.

What makes this workTwo distinct points, each developed with a connective that explains why it matters.
Explain two Sikh teachings about the sanctity of life. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority. (5)5 marks

Sikhs believe God's light is present in every person; this means that every life is sacred and must be protected.

The Guru Granth Sahib teaches that God is the giver of life, which shows that humans should not take life away.

Source of wisdom and authority: Guru Granth Sahib teaching on God as the giver of life: exact verse to be supplied by teacher

What makes this workTwo developed points plus a named source of wisdom and authority that directly supports one of them. The exact source wording is marked for teacher confirmation.
Evaluate: 'Euthanasia should be legal.' (12)12 marks

FOR (1): It relieves unbearable suffering and respects autonomy; this shows compassion, which Sikhs value.

FOR (2): Supporters argue the law could include safeguards such as medical checks, which could lead to fewer people suffering without choice.

AGAINST (1): However, Sikhs believe life is sacred and given by God, and legalisation could lead to vulnerable people feeling pressured.

AGAINST (2): Sikhs emphasise sewa for the dying; as a result care, not ending life, is the compassionate response.

WEIGHTED JUDGEMENT: Overall, palliative care meets the compassion argument without ending life; therefore most Sikhs would disagree.

What makes this workTwo developed points on each side, each a chain of reasoning with highlighted connectives; the judgement weighs the sides and explains why one is stronger rather than simply repeating them. Adding a source of wisdom and authority on each side would strengthen it further.

Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • Sanctity of life is persuasive to believers, but non-religious thinkers give more weight to quality of life and personal choice.
  • Science and religion can be complementary, because the Big Bang can be read as describing how God's hukam worked.
  • The strongest Sikh argument against euthanasia is that life is God's gift, so only God should decide when it ends.
  • Non-religious arguments against life after death rely on lack of evidence, which does not prove that no afterlife exists.
  • Palliative care answers much of the compassion argument for euthanasia without ending a life.
  • Overall, Sikhs balance the sanctity of life with compassion, which explains why some accept abortion when the mother's life is at risk.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Sanctity of life: God's light in all.
  2. Know the Abortion Act 1967.
  3. Euthanasia illegal in UK.
  4. Samsara, karma, mukti.
  5. Humanist arguments: brain-based consciousness.
  6. Environment: God in creation, stewardship.
  7. Spend roughly a minute per mark, so leave most time for the 12-mark (d) question.
  8. In (c) answers, name the source precisely and explain how it supports the point.
  9. In (d) answers, give arguments on both sides, include a non-religious or divergent view where relevant, and finish with a justified conclusion.
