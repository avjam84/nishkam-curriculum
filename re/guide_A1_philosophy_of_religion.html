Concepts

A1.1 An introduction to Philosophical thinking at A level

Key definitionDeductive / inductive and a priori / a posteriori arguments.

Students must distinguish argument types and evaluate premises and conclusions.

Common mistakeConfusing a priori with deductive.
Evaluation pointCan inductive arguments ever establish God? They give probability at most.
Exam linkExpect to explain an introduction to philosophical thinking at a level accurately and then evaluate it: say how strong the ideas are, what critics argue, and how supporters respond. Use precise terminology and reach a judgement.

A1.2 Arguments for the existence of God

Key definitionArguments for God: rational cases for theism.

These include design, cosmological and ontological arguments, each with strengths and criticisms.

Common mistakeTreating arguments as proofs.
Evaluation pointDoes a cumulative case strengthen theism? Swinburne argues so.
Exam linkExpect to explain arguments for the existence of god accurately and then evaluate it: say how strong the ideas are, what critics argue, and how supporters respond. Use precise terminology and reach a judgement.

A1.3 The Design Argument

Key definitionTeleological argument: order and purpose imply a designer.

Paley's analogy and Aquinas' Fifth Way; challenged by Hume and by evolution.

Common mistakeIgnoring Hume's critique.
Evaluation pointDoes evolution defeat design? It defeats biological design but not fine-tuning.
Exam linkExpect to explain the design argument accurately and then evaluate it: say how strong the ideas are, what critics argue, and how supporters respond. Use precise terminology and reach a judgement.

A1.4 The Cosmological Argument

Key definitionArgument from contingency / first cause.

Aquinas' first three Ways and the Kalam argument; challenged by Hume and Russell.

Common mistakeMixing Aquinas' Ways.
Evaluation pointDoes infinite regress refute the argument? Not necessarily.

A1.5 The Ontological Argument

Key definitionA priori argument that God's existence follows from the concept of God.

Anselm and Descartes; criticised by Gaunilo and Kant ('existence is not a predicate').

Common mistakeKant's objection misstated.
Evaluation pointIs existence a predicate? Kant says no.
Exam linkExpect to explain the ontological argument accurately and then evaluate it: say how strong the ideas are, what critics argue, and how supporters respond. Use precise terminology and reach a judgement.

A1.6 Religious Experience

Key definitionA direct awareness of God or the transcendent.

Includes mystical, conversion and corporate experience; evaluated through Swinburne's principles of credulity and testimony and naturalistic challenges.

Common mistakeTreating experiences as identical.
Evaluation pointIs Swinburne's principle of credulity sound? Critics say it is too generous.
Exam linkExpect to explain religious experience accurately and then evaluate it: say how strong the ideas are, what critics argue, and how supporters respond. Use precise terminology and reach a judgement.

Mackie presents the logical problem; Rowe the evidential problem.

A1.8 Theodicies and solutions to the Problem of Evil

Key definitionTheodicy: a defence of God's goodness given evil.

Augustinian and Irenaean theodicies, and the free will defence, each evaluated.

Key definitionAnalogy and symbol as ways of speaking meaningfully of God.

Aquinas' analogy of attribution and proportion; Tillich on symbol.

Common mistakeAnalogy treated as literal.

A1.10 Verification and Falsification Principle

Key definitionVerification: meaningful statements must be verifiable; falsification: they must be falsifiable.

Logical positivists (Ayer) and Flew challenge religious language; responses include Hick's eschatological verification.

Common mistakeVerification and falsification confused.
Evaluation pointIs verification self-refuting? Its own principle is unverifiable.
Exam linkExpect to explain verification and falsification principle accurately and then evaluate it: say how strong the ideas are, what critics argue, and how supporters respond. Use precise terminology and reach a judgement.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Treating arguments as proofs. They are arguments with premises that can be challenged, so they must be evaluated rather than asserted.
  2. Listing scholars. Naming Hume or Kant without explaining their reasoning shows knowledge, not analysis.
  3. Misstating Kant. Kant argued existence is not a real predicate, not that God does not exist.
  4. Ignoring responses. Critiques have replies (e.g. Hick to verification); leaving them out makes evaluation one-sided.
  5. No judgement. A conclusion must follow from the arguments weighed, or the essay cannot reach the top levels.

Answers

Colour-Coded Model Answers

Key: AO1 knowledgeAO2 analysis / evaluationconnective

Explain the ontological argument.Tariff TBC

Anselm defined God as 'that than which nothing greater can be conceived'; this means that God must exist in reality as well as in the mind.

Kant objected that existence is not a predicate, which shows that adding existence does not make a concept greater.

What makes this workAccurate, precise knowledge (AO1) with each idea explained and linked to the scholar or teaching it comes from.
Evaluate: 'The cosmological argument is convincing.'Tariff TBC

FOR (1): Everything we observe has a cause; this means that an uncaused cause is a reasonable explanation.

FOR (2): Contingency arguments ask why anything exists at all; this means that the argument does not depend on the universe having a beginning.

AGAINST (1): However, Hume argued we cannot move from causes within the universe to a cause of the universe, and therefore the argument overreaches.

AGAINST (2): Even if a first cause exists, it need not be the God of classical theism, which shows the argument proves less than it claims.

WEIGHTED JUDGEMENT: Overall, it is persuasive but not conclusive.

What makes this workTwo developed points on each side, each a chain of reasoning with highlighted connectives; the judgement weighs the sides and explains why one is stronger rather than simply repeating them.

Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • The design argument succeeds only if the analogy between the universe and human artefacts holds, which Hume disputes.
  • Hume's objection to the cosmological argument is strong, but it does not answer the contingency version as directly.
  • Kant's critique of the ontological argument is decisive if existence is not a real predicate.
  • The evidential problem of evil is more persuasive than the logical problem, since it only claims God's existence is unlikely.
  • Religious experience is the most personal evidence for God, yet it is the hardest to test.
  • Overall, the arguments for God form at best a cumulative case rather than a proof.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. A priori / a posteriori.
  2. Paley vs Hume.
  3. Aquinas' Ways.
  4. Anselm, Gaunilo, Kant.
  5. Swinburne's principles.
  6. Ayer, Flew, Wittgenstein.
  7. Plan essays before writing: thesis, arguments, counter-arguments, judgement.
  8. Evaluate every scholar you mention rather than just describing them.
  9. Keep the judgement consistent with the argument from the first paragraph.
