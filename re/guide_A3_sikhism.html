Concepts

A3.1 Sikhs and the divine

Key definitionConcepts of God in Sikhism.

Ik Onkar, nirgun and sargun, hukam and nadar.

Common mistakeTreating the Sikh God as only distant. God is nirgun (beyond qualities) and sargun (present in creation).
Evaluation pointIs God better understood as immanent or transcendent? Sikh teaching holds both, and a strong essay argues they are two aspects of one reality.
Exam linkExpect to explain sikhs and the divine accurately and then evaluate it: say how strong the ideas are, what critics argue, and how supporters respond. Use precise terminology and reach a judgement.

A3.2 The importance of the sangat

Key definitionSangat: the Sikh community.

Its role in worship, sewa and spiritual development.

Common mistakeUsing sangat to mean the building. It means the community of believers.
Evaluation pointCan Sikhism be practised alone? Personal nam japna is possible, but worship, sewa and langar all assume a community.
Exam linkExpect to explain the importance of the sangat accurately and then evaluate it: say how strong the ideas are, what critics argue, and how supporters respond. Use precise terminology and reach a judgement.

A3.3 Key moral principles and values

Key definitionCore Sikh ethics.

Nam japna, kirat karni, vand chhakna, sewa and the five virtues.

Common mistakeListing the three pillars without explaining how they lead towards becoming gurmukh.
Evaluation pointAre Sikh ethics universal? They are grounded in the oneness of humanity, which supports a universal claim.

A3.4 The nature, role and status of the Guru Granth Sahib

Key definitionThe scripture as eternal Guru.

Its compilation, authority and use in ritual.

Common mistakeCalling reverence for the Guru Granth Sahib idolatry. Sikhs respect it as the Guru's word, not as an image of God.
Evaluation pointIs scripture an adequate Guru? Sikhs argue that the Guru's word is eternal, so no further human Guru is needed.

A3.5 The lives of the ten Gurus as teachers and exemplar Sikhs

Their teachings and examples shape Sikh belief and practice.

Common mistakeNarrating the Gurus' lives without explaining what each teaches about belief or practice.
Evaluation pointWhich Guru is most significant? Guru Nanak founded the faith, but Guru Gobind Singh shaped its lasting institutions.

A3.6 Sikh Identity

Key definitionBelief, behaviour and belonging.

Identity expressed through the 5Ks, names and community.

Common mistakeReducing identity to appearance. Identity includes belief, behaviour and belonging.
Evaluation pointIs outward identity essential? Some Sikhs see the 5Ks as required; others place inner devotion first.

A3.7 The Khalsa and Meditation

Key definitionKhalsa: community of initiated Sikhs founded 1699.

Amrit initiation, the 5Ks, and meditation on the Name.

Common mistakeTelling the 1699 story without analysing what the Khalsa means for Sikhs today.
Evaluation pointShould all Sikhs take Amrit? It is the ideal, but many devout Sikhs are not initiated.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Narrative without analysis. A level rewards evaluation; telling the story of 1699 earns little without explaining its significance.
  2. Calling reverence for scripture idolatry. Sikhs reject idol worship; the Guru Granth Sahib is respected as the Guru's word.
  3. Identity as appearance only. Identity also covers belief and belonging, so an answer about the 5Ks alone is incomplete.
  4. Ignoring divergence. Sikhs differ on Amrit, gender roles and identity, and essays must show this.
  5. No judgement. An essay without a supported conclusion cannot reach the top levels.

Answers

Colour-Coded Model Answers

Key: AO1 knowledgeAO2 analysis / evaluationconnective

Explain the role and status of the Guru Granth Sahib.Tariff TBC

Guru Gobind Singh declared the scripture to be the eternal Guru; this means that authority passed from a human Guru to the text.

It is placed on a raised throne under a canopy and used in every ceremony, which shows that Sikhs treat it with the respect due to a living Guru.

What makes this workAccurate, precise knowledge (AO1) with each idea explained and linked to the scholar or teaching it comes from.
Evaluate: 'Outward identity is essential to being a Sikh.'Tariff TBC

FOR (1): The 5Ks were given by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699; this shows that outward identity is a commanded sign of commitment.

FOR (2): The 5Ks mark membership of the Khalsa, which shows belonging as well as belief.

AGAINST (1): However, Sikh teaching stresses inner devotion through nam japna, which could lead to the view that identity is first internal.

AGAINST (2): Many devout Sikhs are not initiated, therefore outward identity cannot be the only measure.

WEIGHTED JUDGEMENT: Overall, outward identity expresses inner commitment rather than replacing it; therefore the statement is partly convincing.

What makes this workTwo developed points on each side, each a chain of reasoning with highlighted connectives; the judgement weighs the sides and explains why one is stronger rather than simply repeating them.

    Evaluation

    Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

    • Guru Gobind Singh's significance is arguably greater than any other Guru's, because he founded the Khalsa and named the Guru Granth Sahib as eternal Guru.
    • Outward identity matters as a sign of commitment, but Sikh teaching makes inner devotion the purpose of the 5Ks.
    • Sikh teaching on gender is clear, though debates about women's roles in Amrit ceremonies show it is not fully realised in practice.
    • The sangat remains central to Sikhism, because worship, sewa and langar all happen in community.
    • Guru Tegh Bahadur's martyrdom shows that Sikh commitment to religious freedom extends to defending other faiths.
    • Overall, Sikhism combines personal devotion with social action, which explains its emphasis on equality and justice.
