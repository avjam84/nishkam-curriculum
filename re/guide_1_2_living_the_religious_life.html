Concepts

1.2.1 Features of the Gurdwara

Key definitionGurdwara: 'door to the Guru', the Sikh place of worship.

Key features include the Nishan Sahib flag, the diwan hall, the takht where the Guru Granth Sahib is placed under a canopy, and the langar hall. Shoes are removed and heads covered on entering the diwan hall as signs of respect.

Common mistakeListing features without explaining their meaning (e.g. the canopy shows respect to the Guru).
Evaluation pointAre physical features essential? Many Sikhs say the Guru Granth Sahib's presence, not the building, makes a gurdwara.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about features of the gurdwara; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.2.2 The Gurdwara

Key definitionThe gurdwara as a community centre as well as a place of worship.

It is used for worship, education, the langar, ceremonies and community support, and it is open to all people. Many gurdwaras also offer classes, libraries and space for community events.

Common mistakeIgnoring non-worship uses of the gurdwara.
Evaluation pointIs the gurdwara more important as a community centre or a place of worship? Argue that the two are linked.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about the gurdwara; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.2.3 Langar

Key definitionLangar: the free communal kitchen and meal.

Everyone sits together and eats the same vegetarian food, expressing equality, sewa and sharing. Volunteers usually prepare and serve the food, and everyone sits on the floor at the same level in many gurdwaras.

Common mistakeSaying langar is only for Sikhs. It is open to all.
Evaluation pointDoes langar still express equality if people sit on chairs? Some see tradition as vital, others the principle.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about langar; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.2.4 Nam Japna

Key definitionNam japna: meditating on and remembering God's name.

It may be individual (repeating 'Waheguru') or communal (kirtan). It counters haumai and is one of the three pillars of Sikh life. Repeating God's name is believed to purify the mind and loosen the hold of the five thieves.

Common mistakeEquating nam japna only with silent meditation; kirtan is also nam japna.
Evaluation pointIs private or communal nam japna more valuable? Both are taught.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about nam japna; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.2.5 Prayer and the Guru Granth Sahib

Key definitionGuru Granth Sahib: the Sikh scripture, treated as the living Guru.

Daily prayers (e.g. Japji Sahib in the morning) and the Ardas prayer are central. The scripture is treated with the respect given to a living Guru. When not being read, the Guru Granth Sahib is covered with cloths (rumalas) and is taken to a separate room at night.

Common mistakeCalling the Guru Granth Sahib a book to be 'worshipped'; Sikhs show respect, not idolatry.
Evaluation pointDoes treating a scripture as a living Guru risk ritualism? Sikhs argue it keeps the Guru's word central.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about prayer and the guru granth sahib; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.2.6 Akhand Path

Key definitionAkhand Path: a continuous, unbroken reading of the Guru Granth Sahib.

The full reading lasts around 48 hours and is held for significant occasions; readers take turns so the reading never stops. Families may hold an Akhand Path for weddings, deaths or moving house, and it ends with a bhog ceremony.

Common mistakeConfusing Akhand Path with Sadharan Path (non-continuous reading).
Evaluation pointIs an Akhand Path more meaningful than daily reading? Some value continuity, others regular personal practice.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about akhand path; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.2.7 Gurpurbs and Commemorations

Key definitionGurpurb: a festival marking an event in the life of a Guru.

Gurpurbs include the birth of Guru Nanak and the martyrdom of Guru Arjan; they often include an Akhand Path and processions. Commemorations of martyrdom remind Sikhs of courage and commitment to their faith.

Common mistakeMixing gurpurbs (Guru events) with melas such as Vaisakhi.
Evaluation pointAre commemorations of martyrdom as important as celebrations of birth? Both shape identity.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about gurpurbs and commemorations; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.
Concepts

1.2.8 Birth and naming rituals and ceremonies

Key definitionNaam karan: the naming ceremony.

The Guru Granth Sahib is opened at random and the first letter of the first word on the left-hand page is used to choose the child's name; Singh or Kaur is added. Karah prashad is shared and the Ardas is said during the naming ceremony, welcoming the child into the community.

Common mistakeSaying parents choose the name freely; the first letter comes from the Guru Granth Sahib.
Evaluation pointDoes the naming ceremony mean the child is already a Sikh? Full commitment comes later with Amrit.
Exam linkA 3-mark (a) question could ask you to outline three points about birth and naming rituals and ceremonies; a 4-mark (b) or 5-mark (c) question could ask you to explain two Sikh beliefs or teachings about it, and for (c) you must add a source of wisdom and authority. In a 12-mark (d) answer, use this content as evidence for or against the statement.

Traps

Common Mistakes

  1. Describing without explaining. For 4/5 marks, say why a practice matters.
  2. Langar is 'for the poor'. It is for everyone; the point is equality.
  3. Wrong reading length. Akhand Path is continuous, about 48 hours.
  4. Calling the Guru Granth Sahib an idol. It is respected as the Guru's word.
  5. No divergent views in (d). Show different Sikh views on practice.

Answers

Colour-Coded Model Answers

Key: AO1 knowledgeAO2 analysis / evaluationconnective

Outline three Sikh beliefs about features of the gurdwara. (3)3 marks
  1. Gurdwara: 'door to the Guru', the Sikh place of worship.
  2. The gurdwara as a community centre as well as a place of worship.
  3. Langar: the free communal kitchen and meal.
What makes this workThree separate, accurate points; no development is needed, which saves time for the 12-mark question.
Explain two reasons why the langar is important to Sikhs. (4)4 marks

The langar shows equality, because everyone sits together and eats the same food; this means that caste and status are rejected.

The langar is a form of sewa, as volunteers cook and serve; as a result it helps reduce haumai.

What makes this workTwo distinct points, each developed with a connective that explains why it matters.
Explain two reasons why prayer is important to Sikhs. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority. (5)5 marks

Daily prayer such as the Japji Sahib in the morning keeps God in mind throughout the day; this means that a Sikh lives as gurmukh rather than manmukh.

The Ardas prayer asks for God's blessing for the whole of humanity (sarbat da bhala), which shows that Sikh prayer reflects the oneness of humanity.

Source of wisdom and authority: Japji Sahib / Ardas: exact wording to be supplied by teacher

What makes this workTwo developed points plus a named source of wisdom and authority that directly supports one of them. The exact source wording is marked for teacher confirmation.
Evaluate: 'The langar is the most important feature of the gurdwara.' (12)12 marks

FOR (1): The langar puts the oneness of humanity into practice; this shows that belief is lived, not just spoken.

FOR (2): Langar is open to anyone, so it also acts as a form of public witness to Sikh values; this means that it shapes how others see the faith.

AGAINST (1): However, the gurdwara exists because the Guru Granth Sahib is present, which could lead to the view that the diwan hall matters more.

AGAINST (2): Some argue nam japna is more important than any feature of the gurdwara, because it can be done anywhere; therefore the langar is not essential to Sikh life.

WEIGHTED JUDGEMENT: Overall, the Guru Granth Sahib is the centre and the langar its expression; therefore the statement is not fully convincing.

What makes this workTwo developed points on each side, each a chain of reasoning with highlighted connectives; the judgement weighs the sides and explains why one is stronger rather than simply repeating them. Adding a source of wisdom and authority on each side would strengthen it further.

Evaluation

Judgement Phrases for Evaluation

  • The Guru Granth Sahib, not the building, makes a gurdwara sacred, because a gurdwara exists wherever the scripture is installed.
  • The langar is the clearest practical expression of Sikh equality, since everyone eats the same food regardless of status.
  • Private and communal nam japna support each other, so it is not convincing to rank one above the other.
  • The naming ceremony begins Sikh identity, but full commitment comes later through Amrit initiation.
  • An Akhand Path is significant for special occasions, although daily prayer has a greater effect on ordinary religious life.
  • On balance, practices matter most when they change how a Sikh lives outside the gurdwara.
Reminders

Exam Reminders

  1. Link every feature to a belief.
  2. Langar = equality + sewa.
  3. Akhand Path ≈ 48 hours continuous.
  4. Gurpurbs mark Guru events.
  5. Naam karan: random opening, first letter, Singh/Kaur.
  6. Use the Guru Granth Sahib as a source of authority.
  7. Spend roughly a minute per mark, so leave most time for the 12-mark (d) question.
  8. In (c) answers, name the source precisely and explain how it supports the point.
  9. In (d) answers, give arguments on both sides, include a non-religious or divergent view where relevant, and finish with a justified conclusion.
